{Services that verify a phone number by SMS expose an endpoint that must answer before any account exists. Attackers abuse it to waste the service's messaging budget (flooding) or to steer paid messages to premium-rate ranges that share the termination fee with them (SMS pumping). \section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{M}{ost} online services verify a phone number by sending a code via SMS from an endpoint that must be reachable before any account exists, at a tenth of a cent to twenty cents per message. Two abuses follow from that design. A flooder makes the service send to numbers it does not own, so the service pays for messages nobody asked for; a pumper sends to a range whose operator shares the termination fee, so the service's SMS budget becomes the attacker's income, a technique that MITRE catalogues as resource hijacking~\citep{mitre_t1496_003}. Pumping is the SMS form of international revenue-share fraud, whose voice form was estimated at \$10.76 billion a year in 2015~\citep{sahin2017sok}. The sums at a single service are not small: Twitter's owner said in 2023 that pumping had cost the company approximately \$60 million a year~\citep{commsrisk2023twitter}, and \citet{huh2025ait} found as many attack requests as genuine ones in three months of one large service's authentication logs.

Defenses act at three points on the path of a request. At the source, services limit requests per address, session and device, challenge them with CAPTCHAs, block known proxies and restrict destination countries, and risk-based authentication combines such signals into a score with graded responses~\citep{wiefling2019rba}. At the messaging vendor, Twilio's Fraud Guard blocks destination prefixes and countries whose traffic departs from an account's history~\citep{twilio_fraudguard}, and its per-number pumping risk score advises friction from 60 and refusal above 90~\citep{twilio_pumping_risk}. On a service's own logs, \citet{huh2025ait} detect attack requests one at a time with gradient-boosted trees, at 89.6\% recall and 0.19\% false positives on web traffic, and \citet{zaliskyi2026ait} count application-to-person sends per number prefix in five-minute windows and quarantine a prefix that crosses a threshold.

Each of these rests on an assumption that the literature has tested and found wanting. Source-side limits assume that an identity is scarce; without a trusted authority, one entity can present as many identities as it likes~\citep{douceur2002sybil}. Addresses are not scarce either: five residential proxy services relayed through 6.2 million addresses in 238 countries, 90\% of them for under fifteen minutes each and 2.2\% of them on any blacklist~\citep{mi2019resip}, and carrier-grade NAT, deployed in more than 90\% of cellular networks, puts dozens of subscribers behind one address and makes address reputation ``all but infeasible''~\citep{richter2016cgn}. A CAPTCHA costs an attacker about a dollar per thousand solutions from a labor market that was already paying ten dollars per thousand in 2007~\citep{motoyama2010captcha}. Supervised detection needs labels, which \citet{huh2025ait} derived from traffic peaks after the fact, and a decision taken per request can be probed one threshold at a time. Prefix counting acts on the one key a pumper cannot rotate away from, since it is paid only on its partner's ranges, but the thresholds that stopped 95\% and 63\% of two incidents in the data of \citet{zaliskyi2026ait} also blocked 58\% of a legitimate marketing campaign.

The two closest studies therefore each act on one destination signal, and neither measures what acting on it costs. \citet{huh2025ait} report that about 92\% of attack requests fell on prefixes with zero conversion, against 21\% for genuine traffic, so whether codes are entered is informative; but the signal enters a classifier that needs labeled traffic and was not released because it is deployed. \citet{zaliskyi2026ait} act on send counts alone, without outcomes, and conclude that counts by themselves cannot safely separate inflated traffic from a campaign. Neither reports what a destination policy costs the legitimate users who share a number block with the attacker, neither reports its cost when there is no attack, and neither fixed the setting it would select before seeing the evaluation traffic.

\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}
A service that sees only its own traffic must decide, for every request, whether to pay for a message to a number it cannot verify, on behalf of a client that has no account yet. Source-side controls tax identities that are cheap to replace, supervised detection needs labels the service does not have when it decides, and the two destination signals it can compute without labels, the number of sends to a block and the fate of their codes, have never been compared on the same traffic with the cost to legitimate users made part of the result. The question we address therefore has three parts. On the source side, how much of the flooding that a seven-control baseline admits does a twelve-step redesign contain, at what cost in completed registrations, and through which layer? Where identities are cheap, when does reputation built from verification outcomes stop separating an attacker from the users whose keys it shares, and what does rationing then cost and depend on? On the destination, where a pumper is paid, does counting sends per number block or testing the outcomes of their codes leak less, under which conditions does the answer reverse, and what does either policy cost the legitimate users who share a block, with and without an attack?

\subsection{Contributions}
\label{sec:contrib}
We answer these questions in simulation, inside a complete defense for a registration flow, and we release the implementation, the protocols and every run's configuration and outcome~\citep{otpguard2026}. Everything is simulated: three behavioral parameters have published counterparts and the rest are our assumptions. The main contributions of this article are summarized as follows.
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item We show how an attacker breaks eight assumptions behind a seven-control baseline and close the gaps with a twelve-step pipeline whose trust comes from attestation, signed sessions and the fate of earlier codes (Section~\ref{sec:design}), released as code with a regression test for every defect we found.
\item We measure where reputation built from code outcomes stops separating a residential attacker from the users whose keys it shares, and what rationing it then costs (Sections~\ref{sec:separability} and~\ref{sec:rationing}).
\item We compare a short-window counter of sends per number block with sequential tests of code outcomes, selected at a stated service target, frozen, and tested on held-out seeds and workloads, charging each policy with its leakage, its attack-free cost and its harm to the users who share a block with the attacker (Sections~\ref{sec:pumping}--\ref{sec:fp}).
\item We derive from the rules alone when counting leaks less than testing, and check the estimates against measurements over attacks of up to an hour (Section~\ref{sec:model}, Figure~\ref{fig:boundaryc}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

We group prior work by where it acts on the path of an OTP request, which Figure~\ref{fig:path} draws. The attacker's bots, working through a botnet or behind residential proxies, open the target website and ask it for a code; the website's service endpoint receives each OTP request and decides whether to send; an SMS vendor routes the message; and a terminating operator delivers it, in a pumping attack to premium-rate numbers that a number provider has placed with that operator. Two flows run against the direction of the request. The outcome signal, whether the code was entered or not, returns from the website to the service endpoint, and it is what one of our two destination policies uses. The revenue-sharing line, drawn dashed, carries part of the termination fee from the operator through the provider of the premium-rate numbers back to the attacker, which is why the destination is the one key a pumper cannot change. The works we review sit at one of these points. At the client, the bots and the proxies, are the measurements of what an attacker can buy: residential proxy addresses~\citep{mi2019resip}, shared addresses behind carrier-grade NAT~\citep{richter2016cgn}, CAPTCHA solutions~\citep{motoyama2010captcha,dionysiou2020captcha} and as many identities as it likes~\citep{douceur2002sybil}. At the service are the controls an endpoint can run itself: per-entity limits, CAPTCHA, proxy and country lists (our v1 baseline, Section~\ref{sec:baseline}), risk-based scoring of logins~\citep{wiefling2019rba} and supervised per-request detection on labeled logs~\citep{huh2025ait}; our twelve source-side steps and two destination policies act here too (Sections~\ref{sec:pipeline}--\ref{sec:blocks}). At the vendor are prefix and country blocking from an account's history and a per-number pumping risk score~\citep{twilio_fraudguard,twilio_pumping_risk}, and counts of sends per prefix in five-minute windows with a 24-hour quarantine~\citep{zaliskyi2026ait}. At the operator and the number provider, where the money is made, are the revenue-share number ranges that providers advertise and share, and detection from call records and test-number intelligence~\citep{sahin2017sok,sahin2021irsf}. We close with the gap we address (Table~\ref{tab:related}).

\subsection{Revenue-share fraud}
In international revenue-share fraud, a fraudster obtains ``pseudo'' premium-rate numbers from websites that pay for traffic and generates calls to them; a transit operator short-stops the calls, keeps the termination fee and shares it with the fraudster. The taxonomy of \citet{sahin2017sok} sets the scheme among frauds that persist because call routing is opaque, each operator knowing only the next hop, and because operators are reluctant to share pricing, routes or fraud findings with competitors. \citet{sahin2021irsf} then measured the market from the supply side, collecting 3,149,793 distinct test numbers that twelve such providers advertised between January 2016 and November 2019 across 238 destinations. The ranges are a managed asset: 80\% of them were shared across providers, which subdivide a hijacked block among themselves, and 70\% lay inside valid numbering plans. From this intelligence and from call histories, the authors built a random-forest detector for an operator's call records that reached 98\% accuracy at 0.28\% false positives, where blocking the two-digit neighborhood of a known test number caught 63\%. Both studies concern voice telephony, and the detector needs the test-number intelligence and the call histories of an operator. The lesson for SMS is that the number range, not the calling identity, is what the fraudster must keep, and that a party seeing only its own traffic detects the fraud late. An OTP endpoint is in a weaker position still: it holds neither intelligence nor call histories, only its own sends and whether their codes were entered. That is why the policies of Section~\ref{sec:blocks} are keyed on the 8-digit block and use only those two quantities.

\subsection{Detecting inflated SMS traffic}
\citet{huh2025ait} trained gradient-boosted trees on 9.4 million SMS authentication logs from nineteen countries, reaching 89.6\% recall at 0.19\% false positives on web traffic and 91.1\% at 0.11\% on app traffic. The logs came unlabeled from one large service over three months of 2023; the authors labeled attacks inside traffic peaks, as web identities with four or more requests and no successful validation in a day, or app number-and-device pairs never validated, and treated requests outside peaks as genuine. Two models, with 18 and 13 features at three levels (the request, the identity's history, and country-wide aggregates over 24 hours), run per request before the message is sent. Approximately 73\% of web attacks sent four or fewer requests per identity a day, defeating per-entity limits, and destination signals were the strongest: about 92\% zero conversion for attacks against 21\% for genuine traffic, and 91\% of app attacks fell on prefixes with more than 30 distinct numbers in a day against 0.01\% of genuine requests. The same data show the counter-move: in three countries, about 82\% of attack requests validated their codes, which the authors attribute to attackers avoiding conversion-rate rules. Their detector requires labeled traffic and acts per request; the cost to legitimate users is reported as false positives only, and the model was not released because it is deployed. Their data show that destination aggregation is informative; we add a controlled comparison of two label-free ways to act on it per block, from send counts or code outcomes, and of their cost to users sharing a block. We did not reproduce theirs, so we do not rank the two.

\citet{zaliskyi2026ait} count A2P sends per prefix (a number less its last three digits) in fixed or rolling five-minute windows and quarantine a prefix for 24 hours past a day or night threshold. On metadata of two confirmed incidents fixed windows blocked 95\% and 63\% of the traffic, and 58\% of a legitimate marketing campaign; counting unique recipients blocked 2\% of the campaign and none of the first incident. The authors conclude that message counts alone cannot safely separate inflated traffic from a campaign; they do not use verification outcomes. Theirs is a send counter judged on real traffic by messages blocked; ours compares a counter with outcome tests at a service target, in simulation, by what users sharing a block lose; the numbers are not comparable.

The messaging vendors act one hop later. Twilio's Fraud Guard analyzes an account's historical traffic and blocks sends to a destination prefix or country whose pattern departs from it, with three protection levels and claimed false-positive rates under 1\% and 2\%~\citep{twilio_fraudguard}; its SMS Pumping Risk Score rates a number from 0 to 100 from Fraud Guard, risky carriers, traffic patterns and low conversion, and advises friction from 60 and refusal above 90~\citep{twilio_pumping_risk}. Graded destination action is therefore established practice, but the signals and models are undisclosed, so we benchmarked no product.

Taken together, these works establish that attacks concentrate on destination prefixes and avoid converting, that counts alone misfire on campaigns, and that some campaigns now enter codes to pass conversion rules. None of them compares counting with outcome testing on the same traffic, fixes its setting before the evaluation runs, or charges a policy with the registrations it costs the users of a shared block. Those are the questions of Sections~\ref{sec:pumping}--\ref{sec:fp}, and we modeled the attackers that enter codes, fake receipts and build trust (Table~\ref{tab:attackers}) on the counter-move above.

\subsection{What an attacker can buy}
What a source-side control costs an attacker has been measured. \citet{motoyama2010captcha} bought CAPTCHA solutions from eight retail services at \$1 to \$20 per thousand, found that the wage for solving had fallen from \$10 per thousand in 2007 to about a dollar by 2009, and measured a median response of 14 seconds at error rates of 10\% to 20\%; human labor had displaced automated solvers, and the authors conclude that a CAPTCHA limits access to attackers whose business is profitable despite that cost rather than keeping machines out. \citet{dionysiou2020captcha} classify the machine-learning solvers that have since appeared. Addresses have a market too: \citet{mi2019resip} infiltrated five residential proxy providers and observed 6,183,876 addresses in 238 countries and 52,905 ISPs, 95\% of them residential, 90\% relaying for under 870 seconds and 2.2\% on any blacklist; \citet{richter2016cgn} found carrier-grade NAT in more than 90\% of cellular networks, with 64 subscribers behind one address where each receives a thousand ports, and conclude that address reputation and blacklisting become ``all but infeasible'' there. Behind both markets lies the older observation of \citet{douceur2002sybil} that without a trusted authority one entity can present many identities, so a signed session provides continuity, not scarcity. Risk-based authentication, which \citet{wiefling2019rba} probed at eight large services, scores a login from its address, user agent, resolution, time and similar features into risk buckets and asks for a further factor above a threshold; every service that showed such behavior used the address, and none disclosed its procedure. Step~7 of our design resembles it, but scores a request made before any account exists, so the account history that risk-based authentication relies on is absent. Client puzzles~\citep{dean2001puzzles} would add a computational price per request; we did not evaluate them.

These measurements say what an attacker can buy. For a defense they imply that per-identity controls tax identity reset rather than prevent it, and that the question for a residential attacker is not whether it can be told from a person but what rationing it costs the people it resembles. Sections~\ref{sec:separability} and~\ref{sec:rationing} measure that in this design.

\subsection{Sequential tests}
Our block tests are Wald's sequential tests~\citep{wald1945sprt} run as Page's cumulative sums~\citep{page1954cusum}, with a floor parameter that selects between the two (Section~\ref{sec:blocks}). Quickest change detection minimizes detection delay under false-alarm constraints~\citep{veeravalli2012qcd}, and a CUSUM's parameters follow from the error rates it must meet~\citep{nist_cusum}; windowed statistics and the time-uniform confidence sequences of \citet{howard2021confseq}, which stay valid at any stopping time, are not evaluated here, so `testing' below means our conversion and speed tests only, and we report their error rates empirically rather than derive them.

\subsection{Attacks on the delivered code}
\label{sec:codeattacks}
Malware on the handset or a social-engineering call can take a code after it is delivered~\citep{mulliner2013smsotp,siadati2017sms}. Those attacks begin where our work stops: we ask whether a message should be sent at all, and leave the protection of a delivered code aside.

\section{Threat model and design}
\label{sec:design}

This section describes the baseline we replace, the attacker our design must resist, the twelve steps and two destination policies we designed, and the estimates of leakage we derive from its rules.

\subsection{The baseline and its gaps}
\label{sec:baseline}
The baseline, v1, is our starting point, the registration endpoint as it stood before this work: it sends a code to any unregistered number once seven controls pass, in the order of Algorithm~\ref{alg:v1}. A request from a known VPN or proxy address (the list $\mathcal{V}$, line~1) is refused. The platform $p$ is read from a client header (line~2), and a request that claims iOS or Android skips the two checks that web requests face, a reCAPTCHA v3 score threshold of 0.5 and an origin check against the allowed domains $\mathcal{O}$ (line~3). The number's country must be on the allow-list $\mathcal{A}$ unless the request carries the bulk flag (line~4); the body must be within the length limit $L$ and the number not on the exclusion list $\mathcal{X}$ unless the request comes from a test server (line~5). One message per number per minute, five requests per address per minute and the static caps $K$ per platform and country complete the controls (lines~6--8). Each cap is a counter $n$ that is read when its check runs and incremented after every check has passed (line~9), and the response names the control that refused (line~11).

Each control rests on an assumption that an attacker within the threat model of Section~\ref{sec:threat} breaks. We derived the eight gaps below from the rules, not from observed abuse, and we give for each the line of Algorithm~\ref{alg:v1} at which it opens. (A, line~2) The platform exemptions trust a client claim, so any request can send the header for iOS and skip line~3. (B, line~7) Five requests per address assume that addresses are scarce; a residential proxy pool supplies a fresh address per request, nothing throttles a subnet or an autonomous system, and no client identity survives an address change. (C, line~10) A number is sent to without validation, so numbers that cannot exist, numbers on dead ranges and generated sequences cost the same as a real message. (D, line~4) The country allow-list is the only notion of destination cost; premium-rate and revenue-share prefixes inside an allowed country cost many times a standard message, and a pumper targets exactly those. (E, line~8) Every limit is per key, so a wide attack that stays under each cap burns the budget without bound; nothing watches total volume or spend. (F, line~9) A limit checks its counter and increments it as two operations, so two concurrent requests on different instances both pass. (G, lines~4--5) The bulk and test paths skip checks but need no stronger authentication than a normal request, so a leaked flag is a bypass. (H, line~11) Every decision is pass or fail and the response names the control that refused, so an attacker can probe each threshold, stay under it, and enumerate registered numbers.

\subsection{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}
The attacker is a party that wants the service to send as many messages as possible, or as many as possible to numbers that pay it. The first is a flooder: the service pays per message, and the attacker's gain is the service's loss. The second is a pumper, paid a share of the termination fee by the operator that terminates its ranges; it earns nothing on any other number, so its traffic concentrates on the blocks it is paid for. The share is not public, so in Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} we report instead the share at which each scripted pumper breaks even. Figure~\ref{fig:threat} places the two attackers on the path of a request: the flooder, whose aim is the service's SMS spend, and the pumper, who earns on its partner's ranges through the share of the termination fee that the dashed line carries back from the operator to the client. What each controls at the four points of the path, what neither can do, and what the model leaves out follow.

At the client, the attacker runs scripts behind residential proxies in the service's own country, with a fresh address per request, or from a datacenter abroad, or spread at a low rate over twenty autonomous systems. It buys human-like reCAPTCHA v3 tokens from solving services at about 0.3 cents each, so a score threshold alone does not stop it. It mints a fresh browser fingerprint per request, pre-ages fingerprints for hours, or reuses one two-day-old profile; it can present a forged platform header; and it draws numbers at random, walks them upward, or takes them from its partner's blocks. It knows every threshold, the parameters of the destination tests and the timing of the workers, and it sees only the one uniform response that Section~\ref{sec:pipeline} describes.

On its own ranges, a pumper's operator can do what a real recipient could: enter the code it receives, within a second or after a human-like delay, or only when a block's statistic nears its threshold; report deliveries as failed, so that a billed send feeds no test; and verify on hundreds of identity-and-number pairs for ten or thirty minutes to build trusted history before flooding with the same pairs. It can spread over three to three hundred blocks, at full rate or paced to a counter's quota, and it can buy a solution for every interactive challenge.

Three things it cannot do. It cannot forge an app attestation, so a platform established at Step~0 is evidence rather than a claim. It cannot earn on a range its partner does not terminate, which is why the destination is the one key it cannot rotate away from. And it cannot make a stranger enter a code, so a code entered on a block that real users share is honest evidence about that block; what a code entered on the attacker's own number buys it, we measure rather than assume. Attacks on a code after delivery (Section~\ref{sec:codeattacks}) and compromise of the service, its vendors or the attestation providers are outside the model. Table~\ref{tab:attackers} lists the 25 profiles that instantiate these capabilities; pool size, rate and CAPTCHA class are drawn per seed.

\subsection{The pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the implementation as deployed. An HTTP API, run on any number of instances, offers the client five public endpoints: an attestation challenge and an enrolment, a session endpoint where a reCAPTCHA token or an attestation buys a signed session, the request endpoint, which runs the pipeline and returns one uniform body, and the verify endpoint, which takes the code; two internal endpoints, behind a service credential, take the vendor's delivery receipts and drive the timeouts. Behind the API sit the two procedures of Algorithm~\ref{alg:v2}: the pipeline, which runs Steps~0--11 on each request, and the feedback loop, which applies each transition of a send (delivered, verified, failed, undelivered) as one compare-and-set. All state lives in one store, Redis in production and an in-memory twin with the same rules in the tests and the simulator: the sessions, every limit and reservation, the SMS history, the reputation counts, the block statistics and counters, the denylist, the adaptive multipliers and the operating mode. What the loop writes, the next request reads, which is how the outcome of one send changes the judgment of the next, and why any instance can verify any code. One worker per deployment ticks every minute to resolve the sends that timed out and to run the baseline job that sets the multipliers of Step~9 from the pipeline's own counters. Every external dependency, attestation, reCAPTCHA, address intelligence, the HLR lookup, the SMS vendor and the downgrade channels, sits behind an adapter with an in-memory fake, and a deployment reports which adapters are fake so that production can refuse to start with any. The simulation of Section~\ref{sec:method} runs the pipeline, the feedback loop, the worker's two jobs and the in-memory store as drawn, without the HTTP layer, replaces the external services with their fakes, and in the main study hands the baseline job the modeled legitimate rate instead of letting it learn it (Section~\ref{sec:rationing} runs the learning job).

We designed the pipeline so that no message is paid for before every check passes, and cheap checks run first. Trust comes from evidence the client cannot forge, an attestation, a server-signed session, and the fate of earlier codes, though such evidence can be manufactured (Section~\ref{sec:matched}).

A request passes the twelve steps of Figure~\ref{fig:steps} in order until one refuses it. The figure numbers its stages in reading order: its stages 1 to 4 are Steps~0 to~4 of the text and of Algorithm~\ref{alg:v2}, with Steps~3 and~4 drawn as one stage, the web checks; from stage~5 on the two numberings coincide; and stage~12 is the feedback loop. The letters below are the gaps of Section~\ref{sec:baseline} that each step closes. Step~0 establishes the platform from an App Attest or Play Integrity result and refuses proxy addresses (A). Step~1 binds the request to a server-signed session with a fingerprint and a replay nonce, three requests per session per ten minutes (B). Step~2 throttles the address, its /24 and its autonomous system, with lower caps for hosting networks (B, F). Steps~3 and~4, for web requests only, check the reCAPTCHA score and the origin. Step~5 prices and checks the number before anything is paid for: its country and cost class, sequential or narrow-range bursts, liveness through an HLR lookup, and the verdict stage of its block, with the bulk flag now needing an internal credential (C, D, G). Step~6 validates the body and the exclusion list, the test flag needing a credential too (G). Step~7 computes the risk score of Equation~\eqref{eq:score} and chooses one of five tiers (H). Step~8 applies a per-number backoff, sixty seconds doubling to an hour and five sends a day. Step~9 takes the source caps per source, platform and country, multiplied by the adaptive factor. Step~10 sets the operating mode from the hourly budget, elevated above 80\% and emergency at 100\% (E). Step~11 reserves the hourly count, the spend and any destination slot in one atomic operation, writes the state and enqueues the message (E, H). A refusal at any step returns one uniform reply, and every send resolves through the feedback loop, whose per-key and per-block outcomes are what the next request is judged on.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:v2} gives the same pipeline as we implemented it, in the order of the steps, with the feedback loop of Section~\ref{sec:feedback} and the block statistics of Section~\ref{sec:blocks} as its second procedure. Its sets are those of Algorithm~\ref{alg:v1}, and $\theta_w$ is the web score threshold. \textsc{Take} claims one slot of a limit in one atomic operation and fails once the limit is reached; a refusal at any later line releases every slot claimed so far. \textsc{CheckNumber} returns the number's cost class $c$, whether the HLR lookup finds it live, and the verdict stage $v$ of its block; \textsc{Tier} maps the score to one of the five tiers and, for a client without verified history, raises it to challenge if $v = 1$ and to downgrade if $v = 2$; $m$ is the adaptive multiplier of Step~9 and $K$ the source caps. \textsc{Reserve} takes the hourly count, the spend and the block's counter slot together and returns the counter's stage $g$, by which \textsc{Raise} lifts the tier of a client without verified history past the quota (Section~\ref{sec:blocks}); \textsc{Dispatch} sends by SMS, or by push or WhatsApp at the downgrade tier, after a wait at the delay tier; and \textsc{Reply} is the one uniform body. Read against Algorithm~\ref{alg:v1}, the pipeline differs in four ways: the platform is established before anything else and from evidence, every limit is taken atomically and released on refusal, the number is priced and checked before it is paid for, and the outcome of each send flows back into the keys and the destination block that the next request will be judged on. Each limit and reservation is one atomic Redis operation, but the enclosing steps are not one transaction: on the normal path, a refused request returns its per-number claims and counter slot, and the hourly budget is reserved only for a message about to be enqueued, a reservation rather than a completed dispatch; a crash between them can leak reservations (Section~\ref{sec:method}). Address limits and source caps count requests, except that a request Step~11 challenges returns its source-cap count. Every outcome after Step~0, except the challenge, returns one HTTP~200 body after at least 400\,ms, a floor that cannot hide longer work.

Step~7 computes
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
s = \sum_i w_i x_i &+ w_c\,\frac{\max(0,\theta - r_{\min})}{\theta} + w_s\,f + w_m\,i \\
 &- w_v\,v_n - w_f\,v_f,
\end{split}
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where the $x_i$ are request signals (CAPTCHA score, hosting ASN, fingerprint age, session requests, country mismatch, number class), $r_{\min}$ is the lowest conversion ratio over the request's keys (address to destination block), $\theta=0.3$, $w_c=25$, $f$ and $i$ flag a flooded key and one whose codes are entered within seconds, and $v_n$, $v_f$ mark a number and fingerprint with verified history; other weights are 5 to 25~\citep{otpguard2026}. Tiers start at 20, 40, 60, and 80: \emph{allow}, \emph{delay} (a doubling wait), \emph{challenge} ($-20$ when solved), \emph{downgrade} (push or WhatsApp), and \emph{block}.

\subsection{The feedback loop}
\label{sec:feedback}

\textsc{OnOutcome} in Algorithm~\ref{alg:v2} applies one outcome $o$ of a send $x$. It counts the send on every key it carried, the address, its /24 and ASN, the fingerprint, the session, the country, the prefix and the number, and denylists a fine key for 24 hours once that key has 50 resolved sends and fewer than 10\% of them verified; a verification marks the number trusted, which exempts the client from both destination policies. The block's conversion statistic $S_c$ then takes the increment $\delta_c$ of Section~\ref{sec:blocks} for the outcome, the speed statistic $S_s$ takes $\delta_s$ for a verification, and a statistic that crosses $h$ issues a verdict on the block for an hour and resets both. A send resolves as \emph{verified} when its code is entered, \emph{fails} after five incorrect attempts or two minutes past the delivery receipt, and is \emph{undelivered} without a receipt in 60 s or with a failed receipt; only the first two count toward reputation. A positive receipt after a negative one reopens the send; a late verification reclassifies it, but an issued verdict stands. Each transition records its effects and its time in the same compare-and-set, and a recovery sweep completes the work of a dead process; each effect type applies at most once, and exactly once if the sweep runs within 20 minutes. Reputation counts land in the transition's hour, and a block keeps its latest outage failure time; however, a block's statistic accumulates in processing order, and a verdict is dated at the event that completes the crossing; thus, a late event can backdate a verdict that contains later evidence, moving its expiry and the next test's start (by 10 min in one diagnostic). Simulated runs apply events in time order, but concurrent live instances may not, because a transition is committed before its effects apply; thus, the same dependence holds live unless a block's effects are serialized.

\subsection{Destination policies}
\label{sec:blocks}

A pumper is confined to its partner's ranges but can rotate numbers and blocks within them. Destination policies are keyed on the 8-digit block (10\,000 numbers); therefore, protection weakens as a pumper spreads.

\subsubsection{Sequential tests}
The conversion statistic assumes that users verify with $p_\ell = 0.8$ and a flooder with $p_a = 0.1$: a failure adds $\ln\frac{1-p_a}{1-p_\ell} = 1.50$, a verification $\ln\frac{p_a}{p_\ell} = -2.08$. The speed statistic assumes that a machine enters a verified code within five seconds with $q_a = 0.9$ and a person with $q_\ell = 0.2$, because autofill removes the keystroke~\citep{google_smsretriever,wicg_webotp}; it adds $1.50$ or $-2.08$. A verdict is issued, and the statistic is reset when either exceeds $h = \ln 1000 = 6.91$. The floor $S_t = \max(-ch,\, S_{t-1} + \text{increment})$ sets the memory: unbounded $c$ is Wald's test, $c = 0$ Page's cumulative sum; the default is $c = 1$. A first verdict makes the block's first-time clients pass a challenge for an hour; a second within the hour moves them off SMS, refusing those without another channel.

\subsubsection{Counters}
A counter limits the number of SMS sent per block in a daily or short refilling window. Step~11 reserves a slot and decides the stage in one atomic operation when an SMS is about to be sent, so concurrent requests cannot skip the challenge. Past the limit, a graded counter challenges first-time clients, and past twice the limit moves them off SMS; a refusing counter refuses. Clients with verified history are exempt from both policies; therefore, a code entered on a pumper's number can buy that number an exemption. We also explored a token bucket of the same rate (burst 4 or 12; its challenge-tier bucket refills independently, unlike RFC~2697's~\citep{rfc2697}) and the counter combined with the tests.

\subsection{Capacity controls}

Step~9 multiplies its caps by 0.25 to 1.5 according to a baseline job comparing each key with its expected rate, learned from the pipeline's hourly counters; without history, the static cap stands. Verified history avoids adaptive reduction, not the static caps or the budget, which bounds SMS count and spend, not vendor or fallback costs.

\subsection{Leakage estimates}
\label{sec:model}

We compare the SMS sent to a pumper's number over an attack of $T \le 60$ minutes, by a pumper that solves no challenge and holds no verified history. Against the sequential tests, one reaching $B$ blocks with $N$ requests at rate $\lambda$ leaks about
\begin{equation}
L \approx \min\bigl(N,\; kB + \lambda\,\tau\bigr),
\label{eq:leak}
\end{equation}
before its first verdict, where $k = \lceil h/1.50 \rceil = 5$ and $\tau$, the time a send stays unresolved, is the resolution timeout plus the delivery delay plus half the timeout worker's period (2.55 minutes) if the carrier never verifies, and seconds if it verifies at once. Judged, such a pumper is held for the verdict's hour; therefore, for $T \le 60$ Equation~\eqref{eq:leak} also estimates the attack's total, ignoring prior credit and outage suspension. A carrier verifying a share $\sigma$ of codes with a human-like delay gives the conversion statistic an expected increment of $\sigma(-2.08) + (1-\sigma)(1.50)$, zero at $\sigma^{*} = 0.42$; above it, the statistic drifts away from a verdict.

A counter of $q$ sends per block opens a window with the block's first reserved SMS and closes it after the window length. A pumper that finds its blocks' counters empty (a cold start) sends at most
\begin{equation}
L_c \le \min\bigl(N,\; q B w\bigr), \qquad w = \lceil T / \text{window} \rceil,
\label{eq:counter}
\end{equation}
whatever its carrier does with the codes. If a legitimate send has already opened a window on block $b$, using $c_b \ge 1$ slots, the pumper can also take the remaining $(q-c_b)^+$ before at most $w$ further windows open, so in general
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
L_c &\le \min\Bigl(N,\; q B w + \textstyle\sum_{b\ \mathrm{open}} (q - c_b)^+\Bigr) \\
    &\le \min\bigl(N,\; B\,(q w + q - 1)\bigr).
\end{split}
\label{eq:counterwarm}
\end{equation}
Solving challenges doubles these bounds, and a verified history exempts a client from them. While neither policy's estimate reaches $N$, the cold-start bound lies below the tests' estimate when
\begin{equation}
B\,(q w - k) < \lambda\tau,
\label{eq:cross}
\end{equation}
and a warm start adds the residual quota to the left side. Equation~\eqref{eq:cross} is the algebraic crossing of two simplified quantities, neither of which is an empirically established switching point or a necessary condition: other limits can hold either policy below its estimate. A carrier that games outcomes may never be judged, so the tests' leakage approaches $N$.

In the code, every send takes a slot, verified clients' included. If a block's sends arrive as a Poisson stream with mean $m$ per window and all reach the counter, a window holds $1 + X$ of them, $X \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(m)$, and the idealized share beyond the quota is $E[(1+X-q)^+]/(1+m)$: 7.3\% at $m = q/2$ and 27\% at $m = q$ for $q = 4$. This is not a challenge rate: exempt clients take slots without challenge, and challenge failures, the second tier and earlier gates are ignored. The estimates use only the rules, configuration, and attack rate; what an excess send costs a user needs the simulation.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
We implemented the pipeline of Algorithm~\ref{alg:v2} in about 4\,000 lines of Python on in-memory and Redis backends. Up to release 2.8.3, the in-memory store that our simulator uses let a sorted set expire a fixed time after its first write, whereas Redis kept it while written to, so the simulated outage detector and Step~5 lost recent observations. Release 2.9.0 follows Redis in every rule we compared, which differential tests check, and we reran every study with it; in earlier releases we repaired seven replay and concurrency defects, each with a regression test. Of our 453 tests, 19 run against a real \texttt{redis-server} and 18 exercise specific interleavings of two instances, not all. Step~11 is still not one transaction: a crash after its reservations leaks reserved slots or resolves the send as undelivered. The implementation is a research prototype.

\subsection{Simulation}
\label{sec:sim}
We run the pipeline against scripted vendor fakes on a simulated clock, as Algorithm~\ref{alg:sim} sets out. A seed $z$ opens three random streams, for the users, the attacker and the challenge outcomes, so every scenario at a seed, the attack-free one included, offers the same users, and our comparisons between scenarios are paired; the pipeline $P$ is built from the design $d$ on the in-memory store with the vendor fakes, with a fifth of the user pool seeded as returning account holders. Minute by minute, Poisson draws at the legitimate rate $\lambda_\ell$ and the attack rate $\lambda_a$ set the arrivals, the attacker's only from minute~0, and each request $q$ is drawn in full before the pipeline sees it: identity, address, number, CAPTCHA score, delays, and whether the person would verify. A challenge is answered at once when $q$ solves it; a send queues in $E$ a delivery receipt at the queue and delivery delay and, if $q$ verifies, a code entry after its delay; the timed events are applied before each later request and at the end of each minute, when the pipeline's timeouts and its adaptive job also run. After the last minute the queue is drained through the receipt grace, the resolution timeout, the slowest entry and a resend. The run record $R$ holds the messages leaked and offered per minute, each user's funnel by cohort, the verdict events, the attacker's verifications, challenges solved and bill, and the configuration with the workload digest and the code hash. After a warm-up of ten minutes (30 or 130 for pumping), the attack runs over legitimate traffic at 20 requests per minute. Our simulated users verify 80\% of codes after a lognormal delay (median 22\,s; 2\,s for the 20\% using autofill); 70\% of numbers are reachable on WhatsApp; 90\% of users complete a challenge; 20\% are returning account holders with a stable fingerprint, a trusted number and verified history, so our results for returning users describe trusted identities only.

\subsection{The two protocols}
\label{sec:protocols}
We wrote two protocols and committed each before its runs; a supplementary map (\texttt{results/\allowbreak study\_map.md}) gives each study's seeds. The first fixed the matched comparison and a robustness study over 12 Latin-hypercube points (conversion 0.6--0.9 and five other behavioral parameters) and a held-out family (60 to 120 attack requests a minute, 50\,000 to 500\,000 addresses, heterogeneous legitimate traffic), with claims C1--C5 that we count as holding if true in 90\% of cells, a benchmark convention, not a confidence level. The matched comparison changes only the destination policy and, at three block densities, selects on tuning seeds the least leakage summed over four concentrated pumpers among settings at or above the \emph{tuning target}, the default's completion minus 0.5 points. The robustness study's first run failed C1 in 32 of 108 cells because attack and no-attack runs drew different users; we fixed the pairing, a fix that followed a result, and reran every study. We wrote the second protocol (claims K1--K5) after the matched comparison had selected three counters and committed it before its runs; six of its seed values had served other robustness points, never selection. We reran both unchanged with 2.9.0 (9\,919, 7\,925 and 240 runs, each carrying its code hash); against 2.8.0, total leakage changed in 855 of 17\,964 comparable runs, but the principal selections, eligibility decisions and claim counts did not; one auxiliary setting (false alarms matched at unbounded credit) moved from $T1000$ to $T300$ under both protocols (artifact change report).

\subsection{Designs and metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
We evaluate seven designs: \textbf{v1}, \textbf{v1-corrected} (attested platform), \textbf{budget-only} (sessions, static caps, budget), \textbf{block-limit} (a refusing counter of five sends per block per day), \textbf{conversion-only} and \textbf{speed-only} (v2 minus one block test), and \textbf{v2}. We call a seed \emph{contained} when leakage stays at most 5\% of the attack rate from some minute to the window's end, for five minutes or more. \emph{Attributable} loss compares the same requests with the policy enforced and absent, net of the reverse, in points of offered users: on attack-free traffic we call it the \emph{benign cost}, on the attacked trace the \emph{extra harm} relative to the attacked no-policy arm. \emph{Degradation} compares the attacked trace with attack-free operation under the same policy.

\subsection{What each layer does}
\label{sec:rq2}

In the ablation grid (90 effects, each conditional on all other layers, unadjusted intervals; artifact), removing the adaptive caps lets the farm leak 310 [194, 421] more messages; the risk engine, feedback loop, and block key hold the walk by 166, 152, and 121 messages, and the loop holds the reused profile by 138. \subsection{Where reputation stops separating}
\label{sec:separability}

A farmed-CAPTCHA attacker uses an address and fingerprint once and shares its country, prefix, and residential ASN with every legitimate user; thus, its failures penalize everyone: over an hour with caps lifted (five seeds), up to twice the legitimate rate, 99 to 100\% of it leaks, since a shared key stays above 0.3 conversion until failures outnumber legitimate sends about 1.7 to one. At ten times, 70\% leaks while 17\% of legitimate users are challenged and 3.2\% refused (claim C2, at least 90\% leaked with caps lifted, held in 34 of 36 cells). No single score change separates better: a fresh-fingerprint weight of 40 stops it only by challenging 47\% of legitimate users. In the settings run, reputation separated an attacker only on keys its sends dominated~\citep{douceur2002sybil}.

\subsection{What rationing costs}
\label{sec:rationing}

When separation fails, the cap is the lever: over ten seeds, the default lets 47\% of the farm's requests through and refuses 41\% of all legitimate users, the tightest setting 20\% and 64\%. Both assume an oracle job that is told the legitimate rate and runs every minute; running every five minutes or hourly, it lets 75\%--96\% through. Learning the rate from the pipeline's counters, the deployed job leaks 193 [161, 229] messages with a clean three-week profile, 340 [239, 441] more on a cold start, 106 [62, 151] more with a stale profile, and 45 [10, 77] more with a poisoned one.




\subsection{Pumping against the tests}
\label{sec:pumping}

We ran concentrated pumpers on three blocks with caps lifted over ten seeds, about 574 requests in 20 minutes, and recorded the messages leaked, the runs in which the tests contained the attack, the minutes to containment and to the first verdict, and the codes the carrier entered. A carrier that never verifies leaked 570 [419, 736] messages under v1, which contains nothing, and 90 [71, 114] under the v2 default, which contained all ten runs at minute 3.5 [3.1, 3.9] after a first verdict at minute 3.0; a carrier that buys every challenge solution leaked 106 [87, 127] and was contained in all ten runs at minute 4.3. The speed test catches a carrier verifying within a second at 19 [17, 21] messages, contained at minute 1.4 after 19 codes entered, while the carrier verifying 60\% of its codes with human-like delay was contained in none of the ten 20-minute runs: it entered 272 codes, its first verdict came at minute 11.6 on average and 452 [365, 539] messages leaked; in an hour, 1\,067 [839, 1\,310] leaked and two of ten runs were contained, at minute 47.0 on average. Claim C3 (containment with at most 30\% leaked) held in 36 of 36 cells.

Across 18 spread configurations, leakage follows the blocks touched: with 30 blocks, five to seven runs in ten are still contained.

\subsection{The cost to legitimate users}
\label{sec:fp}

On one block, 20\,000 Monte Carlo trials per cell give the probability of a verdict within 30 legitimate sends: 0.09\% [0.06, 0.14] at 80\% conversion with 20\% autofill, 1.9\% at 65\% and 19.5\% at 50\%. Over 24 hours, the default raises 0.5 verdict events a day on 200 busy blocks at 80\% conversion and 6.5 at 65\%, where they meet 30 of about 28\,800 requests (claim C4 held in 18 of 18 cells).


A poisoner flooding for 20 min the 20 blocks legitimate users concentrate on (caps lifted, ten seeds) raises 16 verdict events meeting 98 legitimate requests, 33 never completing; against observe-only operation on the same trace, enforcement costs 12.7 [4.6, 22.9] completions, 3.1\% of users. One that stops after ten minutes leaves verdicts meeting 406.5 requests afterwards, an attributable loss of 46.2 [16.9, 79.8]. Grading limits the harm; it caps it neither at the challenge nor at the attack's duration.

\subsection{Economics and overhead}
\label{sec:adaptive}

If pumpers earn a share of the 0.1422 USD retail price and pay an event-level bill, they break even at a share of 0.043 under v1 and 0.054 to 0.271 under v2 (the instant verifier 1.310); over 360 minutes under the counter, the human-like carrier needs 1.048. These are break-even shares for scripted attackers under assumed prices, not bounds or criminal profit. On a real Redis (four vCPUs, four workers), sends took 13.8\,ms (median) and 21.1\,ms (99th percentile), and 133 requests per second passed at concurrency 128 with the 400\,ms floor and 50\,ms vendor calls; under an adversarial mixture, 81\% of sends overran the floor and response-time thresholds separated sends from refusals with 88\% balanced accuracy, a separation on that machine, not a deployment accuracy.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

In the scripted workloads, carriers that verify with human-like delay, fake receipts or know the thresholds defeated our outcome tests but not a short-window counter: within an hour of a verdict the counter's allowance, unlike the tests', grows with the windows an attack spans, while over longer campaigns verdicts expire and the tests leak again. Equation~\eqref{eq:cross} summarizes this for pumpers that solve no challenge and hold no exemption; its inputs are in an operator's logs, whereas the cost of an excess send, competition under attack and exemption abuse come only from the simulation. Supplementary Table~S1 sets the seven designs of Section~\ref{sec:method} against the nine source-side attackers on the same traffic and seeds, with source caps lifted so that only the behavioral layers act; each design adds one thing to the baseline, and its differences from the v1 and v2 columns of Table~\ref{tab:headline} say what it bought. Correcting the platform header alone changed only the forged-header attack, from 100\% to 99.5\%, because that attacker is the farm with a header. Sessions and a hard budget removed the single client's leakage (5.6\% to 0.3\%) and cut the premium pumper's from 99.4\% to 94.9\%. A refusing counter of five sends per block a day held the premium pumper and the walked numbers, the two attacks that concentrate on a few blocks, to 1\%. The behavioral layers of v2 removed datacenter rotation (69\% to 0) and most of the reused profile (99.5\% to 22.5\%), and the two block tests differ only on the walked numbers (11.5\% against 13.3\%). No design separates the residential bot (37.2\%) or the farm (99.5\%); those are held only by the adaptive caps of Table~\ref{tab:headline}, at the price Section~\ref{sec:rationing} reports. On the destination, against the never-verifying pumper v1 leaked 570 messages, the default tests 90 and the selected counter 24, and under the shared-block pumper of Table~\ref{tab:attacked} the counter leaked 403 where the default tests leaked 1\,149 and no policy 5\,509, neither policy costing all users anything on average.

The nearest external work, set out in Table~\ref{tab:related}, can be compared only on what each needs and reports, since none ran on the same traffic. \citet{huh2025ait} report 89.6\% recall at 0.19\% false positives for a supervised model on one service's real logs; the attack it targets, a few requests per identity over many fresh identities, is the farm here, which our layers do not separate and the caps only ration, at 54\% of first-time users refused, while what the layers contain without labels are the attacks with a signature in the request and most of the reused profile. \citet{zaliskyi2026ait} report that fixed-window prefix counts blocked 95\% and 63\% of two incidents at the price of 58\% of a legitimate campaign; our graded counter is the same family of control, a ten-minute window on a 10\,000-number block with a challenge rather than a 24-hour quarantine, and it leaked 24 of about 574 requests from each outcome-reading pumper at an attack-free cost of 0.06 points of completion, on simulated traffic with no campaigns in it, and failed its service claims on blocks the attacker shares. Twilio's products report claimed false-positive rates and no leakage. What we report that none of the three does is an attack-free benign cost, a cost under attack for the users who share a block with the attacker, a selection fixed before the evaluation runs and tested on held-out seeds and workloads, and an implementation anyone can run; what the three have and we lack is real traffic.

The limitations follow from the method. We simulated everything and assumed the calibration, and we wrote both protocols for our own generator, the second knowing which counters had won. Some results follow from the generator (a perfect premium list, a datacenter attacker that declines challenges, returning users who always present verified history), and our selection rule used an unweighted sum of four scripted pumpers' leakage and a mean service target met on tuning seeds, which guarantees no service level elsewhere. Close rankings rest on ten or fewer seeds, and costs that round to zero are not shown equivalent. Per-client quotas within a block, carrier spend ceilings, bounded exemptions, tuned token buckets and other sequential statistics lie outside the evaluated policies. Vendor adapters are untested live, and the timing result holds only for the observer tested. Destination policies challenge people for what strangers sharing their block did, and deployment under the GDPR or Saudi Arabia's PDPL needs a lawful basis and a route for refused users.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this work we asked which of two label-free destination signals a service that verifies phone numbers by SMS should act on when a pumper is paid on the destination, and we answered it in simulation, inside a twelve-step defense evaluated against a seven-control baseline, five simpler designs and 25 attacker profiles under two protocols committed before their runs. The design contains the attacks that carry a signature in the request at a low cost to simulated users, while a residential attacker that resembles a person can only be rationed, at a price set by how well the cap tracks the legitimate rate. On the destination, a counter of sends per number block whose quota matches the block's traffic leaked less than tests of code outcomes against short, concentrated pumping and cost its users less, but it failed its service claims on blocks the attacker shares and lost its advantage against paced or widely spread pumpers; estimates derived from the rules alone ordered the two policies in 18 of 18 decisive cells. Every number describes scripted attackers and simulated users under assumed calibration, and close rankings rest on ten or fewer seeds. Replaying real traffic is the experiment this work most needs; the policies left outside the evaluation, per-client quotas within a block, bounded exemptions, tuned token buckets and time-uniform statistics, could then be compared under the same protocols.
