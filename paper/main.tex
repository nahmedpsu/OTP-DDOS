\author[inst1]{Naveed Ahmed\corref{cor1}}

\begin{abstract}
An endpoint that sends a one-time password by SMS to any number it is given will be made to send SMS at its operator's expense, to waste money (flooding) or to earn part of the termination fee (SMS pumping). Starting from such an incident, as its operator recalls it, we present a twelve-step pipeline that derives trust from app attestation, signed sessions and verification outcomes, and evaluate it in a seeded simulation whose offered traffic is fixed before the defence sees it, against six simpler designs and 23 attacker profiles, including colluding carriers. No production traffic was available; every figure depends on the stated calibration. Attacks with a signature in the request are contained while 75 to 80\,\% of simulated users complete (79\,\% in runs without attack). A residential attacker with farmed CAPTCHA scores is not separated at acceptable cost by any signal tested; an adaptive cap holds it to 236 of about 600 messages by refusing 54\,\% of first-time users, given a fast controller with history. Against pumping on three destination blocks, a sequential block test leaks 88 messages, as a closed-form estimate reproduces; a carrier that verifies 60\,\% of its codes, builds trust first or fakes receipts defeats it. At matched legitimate traffic, settings that hold that carrier to 152 messages or fewer raise 150 to 400 false verdicts a day at 65\,\% conversion, and a flat per-block counter holding it to 14 to 59 completes 2 to 17\,\% of registrations on busy blocks.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}

\item A human-like residential flooder was held by rationing; its cost is measured
\item Pumping on few destination blocks: five sends per block plus those in flight
\item Sequential tests and flat counters compared at matched legitimate traffic
\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Most online services verify a phone number by sending it a code by SMS. Each message costs from a tenth of a cent to twenty cents, and the endpoint that sends it is reachable before any account exists. A flooder makes the service send thousands of messages to numbers it does not own; a pumper sends them to a range whose operator shares the termination fee with it, turning the service's SMS budget into its income~\cite{mitre_t1496_003}. Twitter's owner stated in 2023 that the company had been losing about 60 million dollars a year to such pumping~\cite{commsrisk2023twitter}, and Huh et al.\ found as many attack requests as genuine ones in three months of one large authentication service's logs~\cite{huh2025ait}.

This paper starts from such an incident on a registration flow the author operated. The first fix, a hidden CAPTCHA, per-number and per-address limits, a country allow-list and a proxy block, stopped the first attack. The one that came back rotated residential addresses, sent the header that marked a request as coming from the iOS app, and stayed under every threshold. The second design is a twelve-step pipeline with a feedback loop: platform exemptions require app attestation, clients carry signed sessions, numbers are checked before a message is paid for, keys carry a reputation built from whether their codes were entered, a risk score chooses among five responses, and a hard budget bounds spend.

With no production logs available, we evaluate the design in simulation and ask which attacks it contains against six simpler designs, and at what cost in completed registrations (RQ1); which layer is responsible (RQ2); when outcome-based reputation separates an attacker from simulated users (RQ3); what rationing costs when it does not (RQ4); and what tests on the destination number block cost legitimate users under miscalibration, outage and deliberate poisoning (RQ5). The contributions are the incident as eight named gaps, the design and an open implementation, measurements of where reputation stops separating and what rationing depends on, a closed-form estimate of pumping leakage with a comparison of sequential tests and flat counters at matched legitimate traffic, and a collateral-damage study of destination-keyed reputation. Most answers narrow the design's claims.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Revenue-share fraud}
In international revenue share fraud a party advertises a range of numbers, traffic is generated to them, and the termination fee is split along the route~\cite{sahin2017sok}. Sahin and Francillon collected 3.14 million test numbers from twelve such providers over four years and showed that the number range is the fraudster's managed asset, about 80\,\% of ranges being shared across providers~\cite{sahin2021irsf}. They also showed the danger of acting on ranges: coarse blocking has made whole countries unreachable, and their range-based detector had a 5.8\,\% false-positive rate against 0.26\,\% for a classifier on call features.

\paragraph{Detecting artificially inflated SMS}
Huh et al.\ trained gradient-boosted trees on 9.4 million SMS authentication logs from nineteen countries, labelled by a peak-traffic heuristic, and reached 89.6\,\% recall at 0.19\,\% false positives on web traffic and 91.1\,\% at 0.11\,\% on native traffic~\cite{huh2025ait}. About 73\,\% of web attacks sent four or fewer requests per identity a day, so per-entity limits fail; destination signals were the strongest, with about 92\,\% zero conversion for attacks against 21\,\% for genuine traffic. We did not reproduce their detector, which needs labelled traffic. Our question is narrower: for the feature family they found strongest, outcomes per destination key, we make the detector explicit and measure where it separates, what it costs legitimate users, how its memory trades evasion against false verdicts, and how it compares with a flat destination limiter at the same legitimate service.

\paragraph{Defences in practice}
Twilio's Fraud Guard blocks regions known for pumping and destinations new to an account~\cite{twilio_fraudguard}; its SMS Pumping Risk Score scores each number from carrier, traffic and conversion signals and advises friction from 60 and refusal above 90~\cite{twilio_pumping_risk}. Graded action on the destination is thus established practice; its model, memory and false positives are unpublished, and they are what we measure for one explicit design. CAPTCHA solving is a labour market at about a dollar per thousand~\cite{motoyama2010captcha}, and the human route stays open even where machine solvers fail~\cite{dionysiou2020captcha}; client puzzles price a request in computation~\cite{dean2001puzzles}, which we did not evaluate. Residential proxy addresses rotate within minutes and are rarely blacklisted~\cite{mi2019resip}, and carrier-grade NAT makes address reputation on cellular networks ``all but infeasible''~\cite{richter2016cgn}.

\paragraph{Identity and tests}
Absent a central authority, one entity can present many identities~\cite{douceur2002sybil}; a signed session gives continuity, not scarcity, of identity. Our block tests are Wald's sequential tests~\cite{wald1945sprt} run as Page's cumulative sums~\cite{page1954cusum} with a bounded floor; time-uniform confidence sequences~\cite{howard2021confseq} and windowed alternatives were not evaluated. Step~7 is a risk engine of the kind risk-based authentication uses~\cite{wiefling2019rba}; attacks on the code once sent are a separate problem~\cite{mulliner2013smsotp,siadati2017sms}.

\paragraph{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}
The attacker wants the service to send as many messages as possible, or to send them to numbers that pay it. It buys residential proxies at about 3 USD per gigabyte and human-like reCAPTCHA tokens at about 0.3 cents, mints fingerprints for free, generates numbers, and knows the thresholds. A pumper also controls destination ranges through a colluding operator, may enter the codes delivered there, report deliveries as failed, build verified history first, and buy challenge solutions. It cannot forge an app attestation, change which ranges its partner terminates, or make a stranger enter a code. We assume undelivered messages are still billed, which favours the attacker.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

No message is paid for before every check passes, and cheap checks run first. Trust comes from evidence the client cannot forge, an attestation, a server-signed session and the fate of its earlier codes; such evidence can still be manufactured, and Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} measures what that buys.

\subsection{The pipeline and the risk score}

A request passes the twelve steps of Table~\ref{tab:steps} until one refuses it. Step~0 derives the platform only from a valid App Attest or Play Integrity result, so a forged header is ignored. Steps~2, 8 and 11 are atomic in Redis, and a claim is released if a later step refuses. Every outcome after Step~0 except the challenge returns one HTTP~200 body padded to 400\,ms.

Step~7 computes
\begin{equation}
s = \sum_i w_i x_i + w_c\,\frac{\max(0,\theta - r_{\min})}{\theta} + w_s\,f + w_m\,i - w_v\,v_n - w_f\,v_f,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where the $x_i$ are request signals (CAPTCHA score, hosting ASN, fingerprint age, session requests, country mismatch, number class), $r_{\min}$ is the lowest conversion ratio over the request's keys (address, subnet, ASN, fingerprint, country, prefix, destination block), $\theta=0.3$, $w_c=25$, $f$ and $i$ flag a flooded key and a key whose codes are entered within seconds, and $v_n$, $v_f$ mark a number and fingerprint with verified history. Weights of 10 to 25 points are hand-set and listed in the repository~\cite{otpguard2026}. Tiers start at 20, 40, 60 and 80: \emph{allow} sends; \emph{delay} sends after a doubling wait; \emph{challenge} shows an interactive challenge and credits $-20$ when solved; \emph{downgrade} uses push or WhatsApp, never SMS; \emph{block} refuses. Two consequences matter later. The conversion penalty attaches to keys legitimate users share, so alone it reaches only \emph{delay}. A datacenter address with a fresh fingerprint scores about 47, \emph{challenge}, so datacenter rotation is stopped only while the attacker declines challenges.

\subsection{The feedback loop}
\label{sec:feedback}

A send resolves as \emph{verified} when its code is entered, \emph{failed} after five wrong attempts or two minutes past the delivery receipt, and \emph{undelivered} without a receipt within sixty seconds or with a failure receipt; only the first two feed reputation. A repeated receipt changes nothing, a negative receipt after a positive one is logged as a conflict, a positive one after a failure reopens the send, and a late verification reclassifies the send, while a verdict already issued stands until it expires. Speed is clocked from the receipt. Fine keys under 0.1 conversion over fifty sends are denylisted for a day, never a residential ASN.

\subsection{Destination-block tests}
\label{sec:blocks}

Every key above can be rotated except the destination: a pumper is paid only on its partner's numbers. We key reputation on the 8-digit block, 10\,000 numbers, and judge it with two log-likelihood-ratio statistics. The conversion statistic assumes legitimate users verify with $p_\ell = 0.8$ and a flooder with $p_a = 0.1$: a failure adds $\ln\frac{1-p_a}{1-p_\ell} = 1.50$, a verification $\ln\frac{p_a}{p_\ell} = -2.08$. The speed statistic assumes a machine enters a code within five seconds with $q_a = 0.9$ and a person with $q_\ell = 0.2$, because autofill APIs remove the keystroke~\cite{google_smsretriever,wicg_webotp}; it adds $1.50$ or $-2.08$. A verdict is issued when either exceeds $h = \ln 1000 = 6.91$, and the statistic resets.

The floor is the detector's memory: $S_t = \max(-ch,\, S_{t-1} + \text{increment})$, alarm at $S_t > h$. Unbounded $c$ is Wald's test, under which a hundred verified sends postpone a later flood's detection until about 143 failures; $c = 0$ is Page's cumulative sum. With $Y_t = S_t + ch$, any finite $c$ is a zero-floor CUSUM with threshold $(1+c)h$ and head start $ch$, so Section~\ref{sec:pumping} treats thresholds and credits as one grid. The default is $c = 1$.

A first verdict makes the block's first-time clients pass an interactive challenge for an hour; a second within the hour moves them off SMS, which refuses the 30\,\% of simulated users without another channel. Clients with verified history are exempt, and a 24-hour denylist is kept as a comparator. The tests pause for fifteen minutes on a prefix whose receipts, or whose returning clients' verifications, collapse across ten blocks.

\subsection{Capacity controls}

Step~9 multiplies its caps by 0.25 to 1.5 according to a baseline job that compares each key with its expected rate, learned from the pipeline's own hourly counters over the closed hours available; with none, the static cap stands. The default worker runs it every minute, and clients with verified history are not rationed. Step~11's reservation is a hard ceiling on SMS count and spend units, not on vendor or fallback costs.

\section{An estimate of pumping leakage}
\label{sec:model}

Against the block tests, leakage depends on the blocks a pumper touches. With $B$ blocks reached, rate $\lambda$, $N$ requests and $k = \lceil h/1.50 \rceil = 5$ sends per block before a verdict,
\begin{equation}
L \approx \min\bigl(N,\; kB + \lambda\,\tau\bigr),
\label{eq:leak}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the time a send stays unresolved: about 2.5 minutes for a carrier that never verifies, seconds for one that verifies at once. It assumes empty history, no credit, solved challenges, exemptions or suspension, and is evaluated on the blocks each run touched: a consistency check of the per-block mechanism, not a forecast, and once $kB > N$ it only says everything leaks.

A carrier verifying a share $\sigma$ of its codes with human-like delay gives the conversion statistic an expected increment of $\sigma(-2.08) + (1-\sigma)(1.50)$, zero at $\sigma^{*} = 0.42$. Below it, detection is expected with a delay that grows near $\sigma^*$; above it, the drift is away from the threshold and a verdict within a finite window depends on the floor and on runs of failures: delayed, not excluded. Each entered code is a verification on a pumper's number that a registration flow would turn into an account; we count verifications, not accounts.

\section{Implementation and evaluation method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Implementation}
About 3\,000 lines of Python implement the pipeline, feedback loop and adapters for reCAPTCHA, Play Integrity, App Attest, IP intelligence, number lookup, SMS and WhatsApp, on in-memory and Redis backends. App Attest assertions are verified against enrolled keys; enrolment itself is not implemented. Step~11 writes the audit record, counters and OTP entry before calling the sender, so a synchronous callback lands on registered state, and receipt, verification and block-test transitions are atomic read-modify-writes (WATCH/MULTI/EXEC on Redis). Of 273 tests, five run two pipeline instances on a real \texttt{redis-server} and establish, on that backend only, that the hourly ceiling holds under concurrent sends, a number is claimed once under sixteen concurrent requests, six concurrent callbacks for one message count once, concurrent block events issue exactly the expected verdicts, and compare-and-set serialises eight writers; the rest use in-memory and fakeredis stores. No live vendor was called.

\paragraph{Simulation}
\label{sec:sim}
The pipeline runs inside a simulated environment: scripted vendor fakes, a simulated clock, receipts and code entries as events, and a baseline job told the modelled legitimate rate (an oracle) except in Section~\ref{sec:rationing}. After a ten-minute warm-up (130 for the pumping study), each run has twenty minutes of attack over legitimate traffic at 20 requests per minute. Simulated users verify 80\,\% of codes after a lognormal delay (median 22\,s, 2\,s for the 20\,\% using autofill); 20\,\% reuse an earlier identity; 70\,\% have WhatsApp; 90\,\% complete a challenge. Sessions pass the same CAPTCHA gate as the endpoint, and a challenged party retries with the same session and number. Every exogenous quantity of a request, including a retry's CAPTCHA score and which identity a returning request reuses, is drawn when it is created; a test asserts that the trace hash is identical across designs, so designs are paired on one trace. Requests are bound at creation to immutable first-time or returning cohorts, every run asserts that cohorts reconcile and that completed $\le$ delivered $\le$ dispatched $\le$ offered, users are counted before the session gate, the clock advances whatever the pipeline decides, and runs drain with nothing pending.

Prices are list prices, such as 0.1422 USD per SMS to Saudi Arabia. Conversion, delays, autofill, returning share, fingerprint freshness, challenge completion and CAPTCHA score distributions are assumptions, each marked in the calibration file with the published figure it was set against where one exists (one provider reports 68\,\% conversion~\cite{twilio_verify}); seeds bound sampling variability, not these choices. The main study uses thirty seeds per attacker, drawing pool size (500 to 50\,000 addresses), rate (10 to 60 per minute) and CAPTCHA class per seed; sweeps, ablation, cadence, pumping and detector studies use ten, the baseline-job, dilution and 24-hour studies five, the outage study eight. Brackets give 95\,\% $t$-intervals. Runs either lift the source caps or apply them at three times the legitimate rate.

\paragraph{Designs and metrics}
Feature flags give seven designs: \textbf{v1} (header-trusted platform, static caps), \textbf{v1-corrected} (platform from attestation), \textbf{budget-only} (sessions, static caps, hard budget), \textbf{block-limit-only} (sessions, five sends per block per day), \textbf{conversion-only} and \textbf{speed-only} (v2 minus one block test), and \textbf{v2}. The ablation removes one of ten flags; removing the circuit breaker removes Step~10 and the ceiling. We report messages leaked in twenty minutes; seeds contained (leakage at most 5\,\% of the attack rate to the end) with time to containment over contained seeds only; legitimate requests dispatched, delivered, completed, challenged and refused as shares of those offered, for all and for first-time clients; and verdict events, distinct blocks, events by stage, requests hit and those never completed. Each signature attack depends on one or two layers (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}): the session cap for the single client ($+28$ [$+15$, $+42$] without it), the risk engine for datacenter rotation ($+86$ [$+72$, $+100$]), number intelligence for premium pumping ($+76$ [$+52$, $+99$]). \subsection{RQ3: where reputation stops separating}
\label{sec:separability}

A farmed-CAPTCHA attacker uses an address and a fingerprint once and shares its country, prefix and residential ASN with every legitimate user, so the penalty its failures produce lands on everyone: rationing, not separation. Three experiments measure this at the settings run; they do not show that no setting separates.

\subsection{Adaptive attackers, economics and overhead}
\label{sec:adaptive}

A carrier on a listed elevated-cost range is held to 7 messages when it verifies instantly and 20 with human-like delay; spread over all prefixes it is a verifying flooder, untouched at 579. A datacenter attacker buying every challenge solution leaks 54 to 57. Low-and-slow attacks leak at their own rate: 40 at two requests a minute, 188 to 194 at ten. The trust builder's 500 pairs leak 264 while verifying for ten minutes and then 250 of 281 flood requests (162 and 82 with caps on); concentrated in three blocks, its flood leaks 164, because its blocks earn 28 verdict events. Bought trust is not told from earned trust; the floor only bounds how much a block banks. The receipt faker leaks 575 (237 with caps on) with no verdict. Only caps and budget bound the receipt faker and the spread trust builder.

In scenario accounting that credits pumpers with 20 to 50\,\% of the retail price, premium pumping earns 14 to 40 USD per twenty minutes under v1 and nothing under v2; under v2 the concentrated pumper loses up to 1.19 USD when verifying instantly, earns up to 4.55 USD when never verifying and 11 to 30 USD when verifying like a person.

On a real Redis with four vCPUs and four workers, a send costs 12.6\,ms at the median and 19.2\,ms at the 99th percentile, with 48 round trips. With each fake vendor call taking a lognormal 50\,ms and the floor on, throughput is 68, 115 and 131 requests per second at concurrency 32, 64 and 128; at 128, 25\,\% of requests exceed the floor and leak timing, while 99.9\,\% of happy-path requests are still sent. With the floor, no pair of five outcome classes was distinguished by a Kolmogorov--Smirnov test (smallest $p = 0.10$) and all means were within 2\,ms by two one-sided tests; without it nine of ten pairs were distinguished. That is a failure to detect a difference, not proof that timing carries no information.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Lessons}
In the settings run, reputation separated attackers only on keys they dominated; a five-a-day counter was the strongest destination limiter and the costliest on busy blocks; the detector's memory has no free point in the grid; verdicts can be aimed at legitimate users; and receipts come from the adversary's side of the network. Rationing needs a fast controller with history, and block tests need measured calibration.

\paragraph{Limitations}
Everything is simulated, and the calibration is assumed; intervals cover seeds, not those choices. Several results follow from the generator (premium numbers in a perfect list, a datacenter attacker that declines challenges, a farm with human scores), and profiles and defaults were refined against the same generator, a selection risk we did not control with held-out scenarios. Legitimate users make one request each, with no retries or bursts; multi-day attacks and correlated vendor failures are absent; fallback (70\,\%) and challenge completion (90\,\%) were not swept. The main study's baseline is an oracle; the detector comparison covers one block density without matched run lengths, windowed, time-uniform, puzzle or supervised alternatives. About 1\,\% of the concentrated trust builder's pairs share a number. The real-Redis tests establish only the five properties of Section~\ref{sec:method}; vendor adapters are untested live; the timing test excludes vendor latency and 403 responses.

\paragraph{Ethics and privacy}
The design keeps fingerprint first-seen times for 90 days and audit records for thirty, and challenges people for what strangers sharing their network or number block did; a deployment under the GDPR or Saudi Arabia's PDPL needs a lawful basis, a retention statement and a route for refused users. No human data was used. The incident is described without identifying the operator.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this simulation the design contains attacks that carry a signature, with completion within five points of the no-attack rate, and contains a pumper concentrated on a few destination blocks at a cost a closed-form estimate reproduces. It does not separate a residential attacker that looks like a person at a cost a service would accept; it rations, and rationing works only with a fast controller that has history. Carriers that verify their own codes, build trust or fake receipts defeat the destination tests, and attackers can aim the tests at legitimate users. The detector's memory is a measured frontier with a five-a-day counter at its extreme: fewest messages leaked, most registrations lost. Replaying real traffic would show how much of this survives.
