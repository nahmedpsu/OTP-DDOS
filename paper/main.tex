\begin{abstract}
A registration endpoint that sends a one-time password (OTP) by SMS to any number it is given will be used to send SMS at the operator's expense, either to waste money (flooding) or to earn a share of the termination fee (SMS pumping). We describe such an incident as the operator recalls it, the controls that stopped it, and how the attack returned through residential proxies, a forged platform header and per-key thresholds. We then present a twelve-step pipeline that derives trust from attestation, signed sessions and verification outcomes rather than from client claims, and evaluate it in a seeded simulation with legitimate traffic in the background, against six simpler designs, nine attacker profiles, nine adaptive attackers and four colluding-carrier pumpers. No production traffic was available; every absolute figure is conditional on the stated calibration. Attacks that leave a signature in the request are contained: datacenter rotation and premium-range pumping leak nothing, a sequential walk leaks about 70 of 600 messages. A residential attacker with farmed CAPTCHA scores and valid numbers is not separated by any signal we tested; an adaptive volume cap holds it to 243 messages by refusing 51\,\% of first-time users, and only when the cap is recomputed every minute. Against pumping concentrated on a few destination blocks, a sequential test keyed on the 10\,000-number block leaks about five messages per block plus the in-flight window, to within 19 messages of a closed-form estimate at every configuration measured, until the carrier verifies its own codes or fakes delivery receipts. The tests' cost to real users is measured over 24-hour runs, a carrier outage and a deliberate poisoning attack, and it fixed the detector's memory setting. Code, evaluation and recorded results are public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Most online services verify a phone number the same way: the user types the number, the service sends it a code by SMS, the user types the code back. The sending step costs money, between a tenth of a cent and twenty cents per message depending on the destination, and the endpoint that triggers it is reachable before any account exists. A flooder can make the service send thousands of messages to numbers it does not own. A pumper does the same to numbers on a range whose operator shares the termination fee with it, which turns the service's SMS budget into the attacker's income; MITRE lists the technique as resource hijacking~\cite{mitre_t1496_003}. In 2023 Twitter's owner stated that the company had been losing about 60 million dollars a year to operators pumping two-factor SMS~\cite{commsrisk2023twitter}, and Huh et al.\ found that a three-month log of one large authentication service held as many attack requests as genuine ones in the nineteen countries they studied~\cite{huh2025ait}.

This paper starts from such an incident on a registration flow one of us operated. The first response was conventional: a hidden CAPTCHA, one message per number per minute, five requests per address per minute, a country allow-list, a limit on message length and a block on proxy connections. It stopped the attack it was built for. The attack that came back rotated through residential addresses, sent the header that marked a request as coming from the iOS app, and kept every number and address under its threshold. Reviewing the first design against that attacker produced eight named gaps, and the second design, described here, is built around them.

The second design is a pipeline of twelve steps and an asynchronous feedback loop. Platform exemptions are granted only after Apple App Attest or Google Play Integrity has verified the app. Every client carries a signed session token bound to a browser fingerprint, so that an identity persists across an address change (though, as Section~\ref{sec:design} is careful to say, the signature protects the token, not the uniqueness of the fingerprint). Numbers are checked for country, prefix cost class, generated patterns and liveness before a message is paid for. Each network, device, country and number range carries a reputation derived from whether the codes it asked for were entered. A risk score built on these signals chooses among five responses, and a hard hourly budget bounds total spend.

We implemented it and evaluated it in simulation, because no production logs were available to us. The evaluation is organised around five questions.

\begin{itemize}
\item[RQ1] Which attacks does the design contain, compared with six simpler designs, and at what cost to simulated real users?
\item[RQ2] Which layer is responsible, measured as paired differences on the same workload?
\item[RQ3] When does reputation built on verification outcomes separate an attacker from real users in this design, and when does it not?
\item[RQ4] When it does not, what does rationing cost, and what does it depend on?
\item[RQ5] What do tests that act on a shared key, the destination number block, cost real users when the population differs from the calibration, a carrier fails, or an attacker aims them at real users?
\end{itemize}

The answers narrow the design's claims, and the paper is written around that: signature attacks are contained; a residential attacker that looks like a real user is held only by a volume cap whose cost and cadence dependence we measure; pumping concentrated on a few destination blocks is contained by a sequential test on the block, at a cost a closed-form estimate reproduces, until the carrier verifies its own codes, builds trust first or fakes its receipts, and the same test can be turned on real users.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An operator's account of an OTP flood, the controls that stopped it, and the adapted attack that defeated them, as eight named gaps (Section~\ref{sec:incident}).
\item A layered design with graded responses and an open implementation, evaluated against six simpler designs including a corrected version of the first one, a budget-only controller and a flat per-block limit (Sections~\ref{sec:design},~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:rq1}).
\item An empirical separability result for outcome-based reputation in this design: it separates an attacker only on keys the attacker dominates, shown by a dilution curve, a parameter sweep and a cadence study (Section~\ref{sec:separability}).
\item A closed-form estimate of pumping leakage against destination-block tests, checked against observed block counts, with its evasion conditions and the memory setting that trades evasion against false positives (Sections~\ref{sec:model} and~\ref{sec:pumping}).
\item A collateral-damage study of destination-keyed reputation: miscalibrated populations, a carrier outage and a deliberate poisoning attack (Section~\ref{sec:fp}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} places the work; Sections~\ref{sec:incident} to~\ref{sec:method} give the incident, the design, the model and the method; Section~\ref{sec:results} answers the questions; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} states lessons and limitations.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Where the money goes}

Telephony fraud depends on the gap between what a call or message costs to deliver and what someone is paid for terminating it. Sahin et al.\ organised the field into root causes, weaknesses, techniques and schemes, and placed international revenue share fraud (IRSF) among the revenue-share schemes: a party advertises a range of numbers as premium destinations, traffic is generated to them, and the termination fee is split along the route~\cite{sahin2017sok}. They note that the causes, legacy infrastructure, routing where each operator knows only the next hop, and operators' reluctance to share fraud findings, are unlikely to be fixed soon. SMS is a minor topic in that survey.

Sahin and Francillon later collected 3.14 million test numbers advertised by twelve IRSF providers over four years and showed that the number range is the fraudster's managed asset: about 80\,\% of ranges were shared across providers, 57\,\% were mobile ranges, and one block of a thousand Latvian numbers was carved into sub-ranges by six providers in one year~\cite{sahin2021irsf}. They also showed why acting on a range is dangerous: coarse blocking of IRSF destinations has made whole countries unreachable, and their own range-based detector had a 5.8\,\% false-positive rate against 0.26\,\% for a classifier on call features. Both points carry over to SMS pumping and shape Sections~\ref{sec:blocks} and~\ref{sec:fp}.

\subsection{Detecting artificially inflated SMS traffic}

The closest work is by Huh et al.~\cite{huh2025ait}. They had 9.4 million SMS authentication logs from a service with tens of millions of users, nineteen countries, three months of 2023, labelled by a peak-traffic heuristic into 4.95 million attack and 4.50 million genuine requests. Gradient-boosted trees on per-request, per-entity and country-wide features reached a recall of 89.6\,\% at a false-positive rate of 0.19\,\% on web traffic and 91.1\,\% at 0.11\,\% on native traffic; the system is deployed and blocks above a threshold. Three findings shaped our design. Per-entity limits do not work: about 73\,\% of web attacks sent four or fewer requests per identity in 24 hours. The strongest signals were the destination's: the number of distinct numbers sharing a prefix in a day was the dominant native feature, and attacks showed about 92\,\% zero conversion against 21\,\% for genuine traffic. And an attacker would need about 48 times as many prefixes to evade the web model.

We did not reproduce their detector, and we say so: it needs labelled traffic we do not have, and a supervised model on synthetic features would only re-learn our own generator. What this paper adds beside it is narrower than ``a pipeline''. Their system answers block or allow per request from learned features; ours asks, for one unlearned feature family, verification outcome per key, where it can separate at all (Section~\ref{sec:separability}) and what a sequential test on the destination costs real users (Section~\ref{sec:fp}). Their non-peak attacks, on which they report low recall, are the low-and-slow profiles of Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}. We regard a trained model of their kind as a candidate input to the risk score, not as a competitor to the limits around it.

\subsection{Defences in practice and their known limits}

Commercial SMS verification services ship two kinds of filter. Twilio's Verify Fraud Guard blocks verifications to regions known for pumping and destinations new relative to an account's history, at three protection levels with stated false-positive rates under 1\,\% and under 2\,\%~\cite{twilio_fraudguard}. Twilio's SMS Pumping Risk Score is closer to what we build: a per-number lookup made before each OTP request, returning a 0--100 score from ``signals from Verify Fraud Guard and risky carriers, unusual SMS traffic patterns, and low conversion rates'', with graded guidance, send below 60, add friction between 60 and 90, do not send above 90~\cite{twilio_pumping_risk}. A risk score with graded action on the destination is therefore established commercial practice; what the documentation does not give is the model, its memory, or its false-positive behaviour, which is what Sections~\ref{sec:blocks},~\ref{sec:pumping} and~\ref{sec:fp} measure for one explicit design.

The client-side controls that services deploy first have measured limits. Motoyama et al.\ showed in 2010 that CAPTCHA solving is a labour market, with retail prices of a dollar per thousand, 86 to 89\,\% accuracy and median response times of ten to twenty seconds, and argued that a CAPTCHA is friction on the attacker's economics rather than a barrier~\cite{motoyama2010captcha}. Dionysiou and Athanasopoulos classified the automated solvers and concluded that machine learning alone was not sufficient to break text CAPTCHAs, while the human route remained open~\cite{dionysiou2020captcha}. Client puzzles price a request in computation instead~\cite{dean2001puzzles}; we did not evaluate them, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists that as a gap. On the address side, Mi et al.\ infiltrated five residential proxy services and found 6.18 million addresses, 95\,\% residential, 90\,\% relaying for under fifteen minutes before rotating, 2.2\,\% on any blacklist~\cite{mi2019resip}. Richter et al.\ measured that more than 90\,\% of cellular networks sit behind carrier-grade NAT, with up to 128 subscribers behind one address, and concluded that address-based reputation is ``all but infeasible'' there~\cite{richter2016cgn}.

\subsection{Identity, sequential tests and the security of codes}

Douceur showed that without a logically centralised authority an entity can always present multiple identities except under unrealistic assumptions of resource parity~\cite{douceur2002sybil}. That is the position of a web client here: nothing scarce backs a browser fingerprint, so a signed session establishes continuity of one token, not scarcity of identities, and the design's per-identity controls can only tax, not prevent, identity reset. App attestation is the one place the design has a certifying authority, and only for app clients.

Our destination tests are sequential probability-ratio tests after Wald~\cite{wald1945sprt}, run as Page's cumulative sums with a bounded floor~\cite{page1954cusum}. Howard et al.\ give time-uniform confidence sequences that remain valid under continuous monitoring and optional stopping and generalise the SPRT to nonparametric settings~\cite{howard2021confseq}; we use the classical parametric form and report its error rates empirically rather than claiming time-uniform guarantees, which Section~\ref{sec:discussion} lists as the right next step.

Risk-based authentication scores a login from implicit features; Wiefling et al.\ found that every service applying it used the IP address and that single-feature deployments re-authenticated users often~\cite{wiefling2019rba}. Step~7 is a risk engine of that kind applied before a message is sent. The security of the code once sent is a separate problem~\cite{mulliner2013smsotp,siadati2017sms}: those attacks target the person who asked for a code, ours the service that sends it.

\section{The incident and the adapted attacker}
\label{sec:incident}

\subsection{What happened, as the operator recalls it}

We have no logs from the period and give no request counts or bill figures; this section is an operator's account and motivates the design, nothing more. The registration flow sent an SMS to any unregistered number it was given. During its first weeks the endpoint was called repeatedly with generated numbers in the allowed country and every call produced a paid message. There was no limit per number, per address or in total, no check on the body, no country restriction and nothing that told a browser from a script.

The first controls, v1, are in Table~\ref{tab:v1}: a hidden reCAPTCHA v3 with a score threshold on web requests, one message per number per minute, five requests per address per minute, a country allow-list with one country exempted for app users, a body-length limit, a block on connections a proxy-detection service flagged, and per-platform, per-country caps tuned to historical peaks. The abuse that returned did not trip them. Requests arrived from thousands of residential addresses, each used a few times, carried the iOS platform header and so skipped the CAPTCHA and the host check, and kept each number and address under its limit. Table~\ref{tab:v1} lists the eight gaps that review exposed. Two of them, trusting a client header and a check-then-increment limit, are design defects by any standard; the others are assumptions that held for the first attacker and not the second.

\subsection{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}

The attacker wants either to make the service send as many messages as possible or to make it send messages to numbers on which the attacker is paid. It can buy residential proxy access at about 3 USD per gigabyte, a fresh browser fingerprint at no cost, and reCAPTCHA v3 tokens with human-like scores at about 0.3 cents each; it can generate numbers at will and knows the service's thresholds. A pumper additionally controls, through a colluding operator or reseller, a set of destination ranges, may read the messages delivered there and enter the codes, may report deliveries as failed, and may spend time building verified history before attacking. Interactive challenges can be bought solved. We assume the attacker cannot forge an app attestation, cannot change which ranges its partner terminates, and cannot make a real person on an unrelated number enter a code. Whether a message the carrier reports as undelivered is still billed and revenue-bearing varies by contract; we assume it is, which is the case that favours the attacker. Table~\ref{tab:attackers} lists the profiles built from these capabilities.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

Two principles drive the design. Cheap local checks run first, so that obvious abuse is refused before a vendor call and long before a message is paid for. Trust is derived from things the client cannot set: attestation, a server-signed session, and what happened to the codes it asked for before.

\subsection{The pipeline}

Every OTP request passes through twelve steps (Table~\ref{tab:steps}); the first failing step stops it. Steps~0 to~6 are hard rejects on cheap evidence; Step~7 turns the evidence into a score and a tier; Steps~8 to~10 are the capacity controls; Step~11 picks a channel, writes the audit record and answers.

Three details matter for what follows. Step~0 never reads the platform from the header: an iOS request is one with a valid App Attest assertion, an Android request one with a valid Play Integrity verdict, and anything else is web traffic with no exemption, so a forged header is ignored rather than refused. Step~2's limits are each one Lua script in Redis; the per-number claim in Step~8 and the budget reservation in Step~10 are likewise atomic, and a claim is released if a later step refuses. Step~11 returns HTTP~200 with one body for every outcome after Step~0 except the interactive challenge, and pads the handler to a 400\,ms minimum; Step~0 returns 403 to proxies and failed attestations. What that contract hides from an attacker is measured in Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}, not assumed.

\subsection{The risk score and its tiers}

Step~7 computes
\begin{equation}
s = \sum_i w_i x_i + w_c\,\frac{\max(0,\theta - r_{\min})}{\theta} + w_s\,f + w_m\,i - w_v\,v_n - w_f\,v_f,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where the $x_i$ are the per-request signals of Table~\ref{tab:weights}, $r_{\min}$ is the lowest conversion ratio over the keys the request touches (address, subnet, ASN, fingerprint, country, prefix and destination block), $\theta=0.3$, $w_c=25$, $f$ flags a sustained flood on any key, $i$ flags a key whose codes are mostly entered within seconds of delivery, and $v_n$, $v_f$ indicate a number and a fingerprint with verified history. The score is clamped to $[0,100]$ and mapped to a tier at 20, 40, 60 and 80: \emph{allow} sends at once; \emph{delay} sends after a few seconds that double per request in the session; \emph{challenge} returns an interactive challenge to a browser and re-enters Step~7 with a $-20$ credit when it is solved (apps are downgraded instead); \emph{downgrade} may use push, WhatsApp or silent network authentication but never SMS; \emph{block} refuses. The signals are correlated (a datacenter address and a fresh fingerprint often arrive together) and the weights were set by hand and then swept (Section~\ref{sec:separability}); the score is a linear tally, not a calibrated probability.

Two consequences of the weights matter later. The conversion penalty attaches to keys real users share with whoever is attacking through them, so on its own it moves a client only to \emph{delay}, which still sends. A datacenter address with a fresh fingerprint and a foreign country scores about 47 even with a good CAPTCHA, which is \emph{challenge}, not \emph{block}: datacenter rotation is stopped because that attacker does not solve challenges, and Section~\ref{sec:adaptive} shows what happens when it does.

\subsection{The feedback loop}
\label{sec:feedback}

Each send is attached to the reputation keys of its request. A send resolves as \emph{verified} when the code in the message is entered; as \emph{failed} when five wrong attempts are made or two minutes pass after the provider's delivery receipt with no attempt; as \emph{undelivered} when no receipt arrives within sixty seconds of the send or the receipt reports failure. Only verified and failed feed the conversion ratio and the block tests. The two-minute window replaced the ten-minute code validity after we found that 99\,\% of simulated users verify within about 100 seconds of delivery; a late verification is reclassified. Verification speed is clocked from the receipt. A provider without delivery reports is configured to clock from the send, as our first version did, and Section~\ref{sec:fp} shows the difference. Fine keys whose ratio falls below 0.1 over fifty resolved sends go on a 24-hour denylist: addresses, subnets, fingerprints and hosting ASNs, never a residential ASN.

\subsection{Destination-block tests}
\label{sec:blocks}

Every key above can be rotated by the attacker. The destination is the one a pumper cannot: it is paid only on the numbers its partner terminates. We therefore also key reputation on the 8-digit block of the destination, 10\,000 numbers, and judge blocks by sequential tests rather than by the shared-key thresholds, since a block is touched by legitimate traffic rarely (the 24-hour study uses 4, 29 and 144 sends per block per day) and a block with several sends in a few minutes is usually one party's.

Two statistics accumulate per block, each the log-likelihood ratio of attacker to legitimate behaviour. The conversion statistic assumes a real user verifies with probability $p_\ell = 0.8$ and a flooder's number with $p_a = 0.1$; a failure adds $\ln\frac{1-p_a}{1-p_\ell} = 1.50$ and a verification adds $\ln\frac{p_a}{p_\ell} = -2.08$. The speed statistic assumes a machine enters a code within five seconds of delivery with probability $q_a = 0.9$ and a person with $q_\ell = 0.2$, the latter because Android's SMS Retriever and the WebOTP API fill the code in without a keystroke~\cite{google_smsretriever,wicg_webotp}; an instant verification adds $\ln\frac{q_a}{q_\ell} = 1.50$ and a slow one $\ln\frac{1-q_a}{1-q_\ell} = -2.08$. A verdict is reached when either statistic exceeds $\ln 1000 = 6.91$. Both $p_\ell$ and $q_\ell$ are parameters a deployment must set from its own traffic; Section~\ref{sec:fp} measures what happens when they are wrong.

The statistics are floored, which is the detail the evaluation turned on. A plain SPRT lets a block bank unbounded credit: after a hundred verified sends the conversion statistic sits near $-200$ and 143 failures are needed to reach a verdict, so a carrier that verifies for a while and then floods is never caught. Page's cumulative sum floors the statistic at zero and banks nothing, and catches that carrier, but it also turns a legitimate block converting at 65\,\% into a hundred false verdicts a day (Section~\ref{sec:fp}). The deployed default floors each statistic at minus one threshold, $-6.91$: a block can bank at most one verdict's worth of goodwill. Section~\ref{sec:pumping} reports all three settings side by side.

What a verdict does is graded. The first verdict makes the block's first-time clients solve an interactive challenge for an hour; a second inside that hour moves them to non-SMS channels. Clients with verified history are not affected. A 24-hour denylist is kept as an option. The tests are suspended for fifteen minutes on a prefix whose delivery receipts collapse across at least ten blocks, or whose returning clients stop verifying across at least ten blocks; only clients with verified history count toward the second signal.

\subsection{Capacity controls}

Step~8 doubles the wait between messages to the same number up to an hour and caps a number at five a day. Step~9 enforces caps per source, platform and country, multiplied by a factor between 0.25 and 1.5 that a baseline job sets from the key's conversion and its deviation from a four-week baseline of the pipeline's own volume counters; the default worker runs the job every minute, and Section~\ref{sec:rationing} shows that the cadence is not a detail. A tightened cap does not ration a client with verified history. Step~10 reserves the hourly message count and spend before a send and refuses the send if either budget is exhausted, which is a hard ceiling; below it, utilisation above 80\,\% moves the pipeline to an elevated mode and 100\,\% to an emergency mode in which only the cleanest traffic uses SMS.

The defaults and the reasons we chose them are in \ref{app:defaults}; the pseudocode for each step is in the repository~\cite{otpguard2026}.

\section{An estimate of pumping leakage}
\label{sec:model}

Against the block tests, a pumper's leakage depends not on how many numbers it uses but on how many blocks it touches. Let $B$ be the number of distinct 8-digit blocks its requests actually reach, $\lambda$ its request rate, $N$ its total requests, and $k$ the number of sends a block costs before its verdict when every send goes the attacker's way and the block starts with no history. With the parameters above $k = \lceil 6.91 / 1.50 \rceil = 5$ for both tests. Then
\begin{equation}
L \approx \min\bigl(N,\; kB + \lambda\,\tau\bigr),
\label{eq:leak}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ is the time a send stays unresolved: for a carrier that never verifies, the resolution window plus the delivery delay plus half the period of the worker that resolves timeouts, about 2.5 minutes with our defaults; for one that verifies at once, the delivery delay plus its own entry delay. Equation~\eqref{eq:leak} is an approximation under stated conditions, not a bound: it assumes empty history, no credit, no solved challenges, no exemptions and no suspension. Section~\ref{sec:pumping} reports its error at nineteen configurations.

A carrier that verifies a share $\sigma$ of its codes with human-like delay changes the conversion statistic's expected increment per send to $\sigma(-2.08) + (1-\sigma)(1.50)$, which is zero at $\sigma^{*} = 0.42$. Below that share the statistic drifts upward and a verdict is a matter of time; above it the statistic drifts downward and the floor decides whether the block is ever caught. This is a zero-drift point under a Bernoulli model, not a guarantee in either direction, and the bounded floor is what makes the region above $\sigma^{*}$ depend on the carrier's strategy; Section~\ref{sec:pumping} measures one point, 60\,\%, under all three floors. Each verified code is also a registered account on a number the pumper controls, so evasion by verification costs the pumper at least $\sigma$ fake accounts per paid message and leaves the defender holding them.

\section{Implementation and evaluation method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Implementation}

The pipeline, store, feedback loop and vendor adapters are about 3\,000 lines of Python with one method per step; state sits behind one interface with an in-memory backend for tests and a Redis backend for deployment. Adapters exist for reCAPTCHA (including the interactive challenge), Play Integrity, App Attest, two IP-intelligence services, a number-lookup service, Twilio SMS and WhatsApp, push and alerts, each with a stated outage policy: CAPTCHA and attestation fail closed, IP intelligence and number lookup fail open. A FastAPI service exposes session, request, verify and delivery-receipt endpoints; the default worker resolves timeouts and runs the baseline job from the pipeline's own counters. The verification code travels in the message body, and delivery and verification callbacks are idempotent. The suite has 227 tests on both backends, including concurrency tests for the per-number claim and the budget reservation. No live vendor call has been made from the repository.

\subsection{Simulation}

The evaluation is a minute-by-minute simulation of one registration source, with the pipeline running as deployed and the feedback loop driven by the simulated provider's receipts and the simulated users' entries. Each run warms up with ten minutes of legitimate traffic (130 minutes for the pumping study, which includes a variant that needs the history), then runs twenty minutes of attack with legitimate traffic in the background at 20 requests per minute. Simulated users verify 80\,\% of codes after a lognormal entry delay (median 22\,s from delivery; median 2\,s for the 20\,\% whose device fills the code in); 20\,\% of requests come from a browser that verified before; 70\,\% of users are reachable on WhatsApp, the downgrade channel for someone with no registered device; 5\,\% leave through an in-country hosting range, 5\,\% roam from abroad and 2\,\% sit behind a cloud proxy abroad; 90\,\% complete an interactive challenge when shown one. Receipts arrive after a lognormal delay (median 3\,s) plus the sender's queue delay. Sessions are obtained through the same CAPTCHA gate as the real endpoint; a challenged party retries with the same session, address and number; events are processed at their own timestamps.

Prices are list prices: 0.1422 USD per SMS to Saudi Arabia, 0.008 USD per number lookup, 0.001 USD per CAPTCHA assessment, 0.003 USD per solved token, 3 USD per gigabyte of residential proxy traffic. The behavioural figures, conversion, entry delay, autofill share, returning share, fingerprint freshness, challenge completion and CAPTCHA score distributions, are assumptions, not measurements; the calibration file marks each one and gives the published figure it was set against where one exists (one large provider reports a 68\,\% global conversion rate~\cite{twilio_verify}). Every absolute number in Section~\ref{sec:results} is conditional on these assumptions.

The random streams are split: the offered workload (arrivals, identities, numbers, entry delays) is drawn at request creation independently of the defence, so runs with the same seed see the same traffic and differences between designs can be paired per seed. Each main-study attacker runs on thirty seeds, with the pool size (500 to 50\,000 addresses), the request rate (10 to 60 per minute) and the CAPTCHA score class drawn per seed; sweeps, the ablation and the pumping study use ten seeds, the dilution curve five, the 24-hour study five per cell and the outage study eight. Results are means with 95\,\% $t$-intervals over seeds. Two modes are run: \emph{caps lifted}, so that the other layers are visible, and \emph{with source caps} at three times the legitimate rate, adaptive for v2 and static for designs without Step~9.

\subsection{Designs compared}

Seven designs run through the same code with feature flags: \textbf{v1}, the first design with the header-trusted platform and static caps; \textbf{v1-corrected}, v1 with the platform derived from attestation; \textbf{budget-only}, attestation, sessions, static caps and the hard hourly budget, no scoring; \textbf{block-limit-only}, attestation, sessions and a flat limit of five sends per destination block per day, no scoring; \textbf{conversion-only} and \textbf{speed-only}, v2 with one of the two block tests switched off; and \textbf{v2}.

\subsection{Metrics}

For the attacker: messages leaked over the twenty minutes; the fraction of seeds contained, where contained means leakage fell to 5\,\% of the attack rate and stayed there to the end of the run; time to containment averaged over contained seeds only; late-window leakage per minute after containment, or over the last five minutes of an uncontained run, which is a late-window rate and not a steady state. For simulated users: dispatched (a channel was chosen), delivered (the provider's receipt said so), completed (the code was entered), challenged and refused, each as a share of their requests, reported for all users and separately for first-time clients (no verified history) and returning clients; a failed delivery counts as refused. For the block tests: blocks that reached a verdict and the share of real users a verdict touched.

\subsection{RQ3: where reputation stops separating}
\label{sec:separability}

The conversion penalty attaches to the keys a request touches. A residential attacker with farmed scores touches an address it uses once, a fingerprint it uses once, and a country, a prefix and a residential ASN it shares with every real user. Its failures land on the shared keys, and the penalty they produce lands on everyone: that is rationing, not separation. Three experiments show how far this goes within this design. We do not claim more than that: a finite sweep cannot show that no setting separates, and we have not proved an indistinguishability result. What follows is the evidence that the signals this design observes do not do it.

Figure~\ref{fig:dilution} raises the attack from half the legitimate rate to ten times it for an hour. Up to twice the legitimate rate the attack leaks 99 to 100\,\% because the shared keys' ratios stay above 0.3 while real users keep verifying. At five times, 95\,\% leaks while 7\,\% of real users are challenged and 2\,\% refused; at ten times, 70\,\% leaks while 17\,\% are challenged and 3\,\% refused. The penalty starts near twice the legitimate volume and fires on real users as it starts.

What the three experiments say together is that in this design outcome-based reputation separates an attacker only on keys the attacker dominates, and the attacker chooses how pure the client-side keys are. Douceur's argument explains why: nothing scarce backs a web identity~\cite{douceur2002sybil}. The keys where separation remains possible are the ones the attacker reuses by mistake, like the reused profile, and the one it cannot rotate, the destination.

\subsection{RQ4: what rationing costs, and what it depends on}
\label{sec:rationing}

\begin{figure}[t]

When separation fails, the volume cap is the lever this design has, and Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} measures it. Holding the attacker to about 11 messages a minute refuses 38\,\% of simulated requests (the default: adaptive floor 0.25, base cap three times the legitimate rate); holding it to about 3 a minute refuses 56\,\%; letting it through refuses 1\,\%. The frontier is the nondominated set of the configurations we ran, not an optimum.

The gain depends on one thing the first version of this paper did not report. The baseline job that tightens the cap runs every minute in the default worker. Run every ten minutes, the same design leaks 565 of the farm's messages at 2\,\% first-time refusal; run hourly, 575 at 1.3\,\%, which is the static cap. A twenty-minute attack seen by an hourly controller is not seen at all. Whether a minute-level controller on a four-week baseline is operable in a given deployment is a question for that deployment; we state the dependence rather than resolve it.

\subsection{RQ5: what the block tests cost real users}
\label{sec:fp}

The block tests act on a key real people share by the thousand, and they were calibrated to the simulated population. Four experiments ask what they do when the population differs, over a day, during an outage, and when an attacker aims them.

The first is a Monte Carlo of the deployed tests, bounded floor included, on one block that sees only legitimate traffic (Table~\ref{tab:fp}). It gives the probability that the block reaches a verdict in a day, which is a different quantity from the $0.2^5$ probability of one particular run of five failures: the test runs continuously, restarts after a verdict and sees both statistics. With the tests assuming 80\,\% conversion and 20\,\% fast verifications, a population converting at 80\,\% produces a verdict on about one block in a thousand per day; a 30\,\% autofill share against the 20\,\% assumption raises that to under one percent. A population converting at 65\,\% produces a verdict on about 2\,\% of blocks with thirty sends a day and 2.5 to 3.5\,\% at a hundred; at 50\,\%, on 9 to 41\,\%. The conversion assumption is the sensitive one, and it must come from measured traffic.

The second runs the full simulation for 24 hours with no attacker, five seeds per cell, with legitimate numbers drawn from a fixed set of blocks so that each sees 144, 29 or about 4 sends a day, under two protocols: the detector's speed calibration held at the deployed 20\,\%, and recalibrated to the population's actual autofill share. At 80\,\% conversion the day produces 0 to 2 verdicts and touches at most 2 of about 28\,700 simulated users in most cells; the worst cells, 30\,\% autofill against the fixed 20\,\% calibration, produce 5 to 7 verdicts and touch 1 to 5 users, and recalibrating brings them back to 0 to 1. At 65\,\% conversion the day produces 6 to 21 verdicts and touches 20 to 43 users at 144 and 29 sends a day, under either protocol, because the conversion assumption, not the speed one, is what is wrong; those users meet a challenge, and delivery stays at 99.3\,\% in every cell. With the no-credit floor the same 65\,\% population produced 103 to 139 verdicts and touched 700 to 870 users a day, which is why the bounded floor is the default despite what it costs in Table~\ref{tab:pumping}.

The third stops one prefix, a seventh of the traffic, for thirty minutes inside an hour, eight seeds, in two forms: the provider reports every send failed, or reports delivery while nobody receives anything. With receipts gating the tests and the carrier-wide suspension, the outage produces 0.5 to 0.8 verdicts and touches 1 to 2 users; without receipts, 1.5 verdicts and 3 users. The receipts help; they do not remove the effect, and the suspension's conversion signal needs ten returning users' failures before it fires. Delivery during the failed-receipt outage is 92\,\%, which is the outage itself now measured as a delivery, not a dispatch.

The fourth is an attacker. The block poisoner floods the blocks real users concentrate on so that those blocks reach a verdict: with caps lifted it earns verdicts on 12 blocks per run and makes 25\,\% of simulated users solve a challenge; with caps on, 8 blocks and 11\,\%, with delivery at 96\,\% and 69\,\% (the second mostly through the cap). The graded verdict keeps the harm at a challenge; the hard denylist would have refused those users for a day. This is the central risk of destination-keyed reputation, and we have no mitigation beyond the grading to report.

\subsection{Adaptive attackers, economics and overhead}
\label{sec:adaptive}

A colluding carrier on a range the prefix table lists as elevated-cost is held to 7 messages when it verifies instantly (the speed test) and 20 when it verifies with human-like delay (the per-prefix cap); on an unlisted standard range the human-like carrier is the pumper of Table~\ref{tab:pumping}. A datacenter attacker that buys a solution to every challenge leaks 53 to 56 messages per run and loses about 256 session attempts at the CAPTCHA gate doing it. Low-and-slow attacks under the dilution bound leak at their own rate: 41 messages in twenty minutes at two per minute over twenty ASNs, 198 at ten. The trust-building pumper, whose 500 identities and numbers verify everything for ten minutes and then flood, leaks 511 with caps lifted and 349 with caps on, keeping 262 and 194 verified accounts; the exemptions meant for returning users work in its favour, and nothing in the design distinguishes earned trust from bought trust. The receipt-faking carrier leaks 567 and 255 with no verdict ever. Both are bounded only by the caps and the budget.

With revenue credited only to pumpers at a 20 to 50\,\% share of the 0.1422 USD retail price, premium-prefix pumping earns 14 to 38 USD per twenty minutes under v1 and nothing under v2; under v2 the concentrated pumper earns 0.85 USD at the 50\,\% share when it verifies instantly, 5.27 USD when it never verifies, and 29.50 USD plus 264 accounts when it verifies like a person. This is the stated accounting, which charges the attacker for requests sent after containment and takes the retail price as the termination fee; real termination revenue is not public.

Overhead was measured against a real Redis on a four-vCPU container with four worker processes and faked vendors: a full send costs 11.0\,ms at the median and 18.9\,ms at the 99th percentile in process, with 48 Redis round trips, and the service serves 248 requests per second at concurrency 32 without the minimum response time. With it, 6\,000 requests across five outcome classes including the challenge showed no pair that a Kolmogorov--Smirnov test distinguished (smallest $p = 0.32$) and every pair's mean within 2\,ms by a two one-sided test (largest difference 0.22\,ms); without it, nine of ten pairs are distinguished. At this sample size and with faked vendor latency, one response time tells an attacker nothing about the outcome; a larger sample, real vendor latency or another statistic might.

\section{Discussion: lessons and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Lessons}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{In this design, reputation separates attackers only on keys they dominate.} The dilution curve, the sweep and the cadence study all say so, and Douceur's argument says why. We state it as an empirical result for the signals this design observes, not as a theorem.
\item \emph{A counter is enough for a pumper that never verifies.} The flat per-block limit matched or beat the sequential tests on the walk and the premium range. The tests pay for themselves only against carriers that enter codes, and their memory setting decides which of those they catch.
\item \emph{The detector's memory is a trade between a verifying carrier and legitimate blocks.} No credit catches the 60\,\% verifier and flags a 65\,\% block a hundred times a day; one threshold of credit does the reverse. There is no setting that does both, and the choice belongs to the deployment.
\item \emph{A wrong verdict on a shared key must be graded, and an attacker can cause one.} The poisoner made a quarter of simulated users solve a challenge. Grading kept it at that.
\item \emph{Failure is a delivery receipt and a timeout, and the receipt comes from the adversary's side of the network.} Clocking from the receipt removed the outage false positives the send clock produced; a carrier that fakes receipts removes the detector.
\item \emph{Rationing needs a fast controller, and the block tests need a measured calibration.} The adaptive cap's effect came entirely from a minute-level job; the tests assume a conversion rate and an autofill share, and set wrong they flag real blocks at the rates of Table~\ref{tab:fp}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

Everything is simulated, and the behavioural calibration is assumed, not validated: conversion, entry delays, autofill and returning shares, fingerprint freshness, CAPTCHA score distributions and challenge completion are all choices, and the confidence intervals cover seeds, not those choices. No logs from the incident or the v1 period exist in our hands; the repository has a replay tool and a log schema for when they do, and that replay is the experiment this paper most needs. The number-lookup fake says every number is live. The prefix table is a sample. Legitimate behaviour is stylised: one request per user, no retries after a failed delivery, every user on the attacker's ISP, no synchronised bursts, no multi-day attacks, no device farms. The adaptive controller's four-week baseline is replaced by the known legitimate rate. Non-SMS channels are assumed available for 70\,\% of users; for the rest a downgrade is a refusal. Attackers do not adapt within a run except through the adaptive profiles, and two of those, the receipt-faking carrier and the trust builder, defeat the block tests outright. The paired ablation measures marginal, not total, contributions. We did not evaluate client puzzles, a sliding-window or change-detection alternative to the cumulative statistic, a supervised model, or a time-uniform test of the kind Howard et al.\ give; each is a sensible comparison we lack. The timing measurement excludes vendor latency and Step~0's 403 responses. The leakage estimate is conditional on empty history, no credit and no exemptions, and its error is reported, not bounded.

\subsection{Ethics and privacy}

The design hashes browser attributes and keeps the hash's first-seen time for 90 days; keeps addresses and numbers in reputation counters for a day and in audit records for thirty; keeps the set of numbers that verified once until it is cleared; and challenges people on the strength of what strangers on their network or in their number block did. Each is a choice with a cost, and a deployment under the GDPR or Saudi Arabia's PDPL needs a lawful basis, a retention statement and a route for a refused person to reach someone. No human data was used; the simulated users are synthetic. The incident is described from the operator's recollection without identifying the operator, its product or its country beyond the number ranges in the sample configuration, and the repository records that written permission to describe it is being sought.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

An OTP endpoint is an SMS budget behind a form, and the first thing that stops a flood is not the last. In the simulation reported here, the design contains the attacks that leave a signature in the request at no measured cost to simulated users, and it contains a pumper concentrated on a few destination blocks at a cost that a closed-form estimate reproduces to within 19 messages across nineteen configurations. It does not separate a residential attacker that looks like a person: no signal in the sweep did, the only containment is a volume cap that refuses half of first-time users, and that cap works only when recomputed every minute. The destination tests are defeated by a carrier that verifies enough of its own codes, builds trust first or fakes its receipts, and they can be turned on real users by an attacker who floods their blocks. Their memory setting is a measured trade between a verifying carrier and false verdicts on legitimate blocks. Those are the results; the artifact lets anyone rerun them, and replaying real traffic through it is the experiment that would decide how much of this survives contact with a real population.
