\author[inst1]{Naveed Ahmed\corref{cor1}}

\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}

\begin{abstract}
An endpoint that sends a one-time password by SMS to any number it is given will be abused, to waste money (flooding) or to earn part of the termination fee (SMS pumping). Starting from such an incident, as its operator recalls it, we present a twelve-step pipeline that derives trust from app attestation, signed sessions and verification outcomes, and evaluate it in simulation, with paired legitimate traffic, against six simpler designs and 25 attacker profiles. A protocol committed before the main runs fixed comparator selection and five robustness claims; all hold, after a pairing defect that failed one claim in the first run was repaired. Attacks with a signature in the request are contained with completion within two points of the no-attack run. A residential attacker with farmed CAPTCHA scores was not separated by any tested signal without challenging nearly half of legitimate users; an adaptive cap held it to 46\% of its requests by refusing 54\% of first-time users. Sequential tests of verification outcomes on three pumped destination blocks leaked 89 of about 560 messages, but 405 when the carrier verified 60\% of its codes. At a matched service target, a short-window counter of SMS sends per block leaked 6 to 48 messages against every pumper tested, at an attributable completion loss of 0.06 to 0.43\% of users.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Most online services verify a phone number by sending it a code by SMS, from an endpoint reachable before any account exists, at a tenth of a cent to twenty cents a message. A flooder makes the service send to numbers it does not own; a pumper sends to a range whose operator shares the termination fee, turning the service's SMS budget into its income~\cite{mitre_t1496_003}. Twitter's owner said in 2023 that pumping had cost the company about 60 million dollars a year~\cite{commsrisk2023twitter}, and Huh et al.\ found as many attack requests as genuine ones in three months of one large service's logs~\cite{huh2025ait}.

This paper starts from such an incident on a registration flow the author operated. A hidden CAPTCHA, per-number and per-address limits, a country allow-list and a proxy block stopped the first attack. The one that came back rotated residential addresses, sent the header that marked a request as coming from the iOS app, and stayed under every threshold. The redesign is a twelve-step pipeline: attestation-backed platform exemptions, signed sessions, number checks before a message is paid for, key reputation built from whether codes were entered, a risk score choosing among five responses, and a hard budget.

We evaluate it in simulation under a protocol committed before the main runs, asking which attacks it contains against simpler designs, and at what cost in completed registrations (RQ1); which layer is responsible (RQ2); when outcome-based reputation separates an attacker from simulated users (RQ3); what rationing costs when it does not (RQ4); and what destination policies cost legitimate users (RQ5). We contribute the incident as eight named gaps, the design and an open implementation, measurements of where reputation stops separating and what rationing depends on, a closed-form leakage estimate, a matched comparison of destination policies, and a collateral-damage study. The most useful finding is negative for our own detector: on the one key a pumper cannot rotate, counting SMS sends did better overall than testing what happened to them.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Revenue-share fraud}
In international revenue share fraud, traffic is generated to an advertised number range and the termination fee is split along the route~\cite{sahin2017sok}. Sahin and Francillon collected 3.14 million test numbers from twelve providers over four years; the range is the fraudster's managed asset, about 80\% of ranges being shared across providers~\cite{sahin2021irsf}. Acting on ranges is risky: their range-based detector had 5.8\% false positives against 0.26\% for a classifier on call features.

\paragraph{Detecting artificially inflated SMS}
Huh et al.\ trained gradient-boosted trees on 9.4 million SMS authentication logs from nineteen countries, reaching 89.6\% recall at 0.19\% false positives on web traffic and 91.1\% at 0.11\% on native traffic~\cite{huh2025ait}. About 73\% of web attacks sent four or fewer requests per identity a day, defeating per-entity limits; destination signals were the strongest, with about 92\% zero conversion for attacks against 21\% for genuine traffic. Their detector learns request, entity and country features from labelled traffic and acts per request; ours hand-specifies one feature family, outcomes and volume per destination block, and acts per block. We did not reproduce theirs, and ours is one transparent, evaluated instance, not a representative of the family.

\paragraph{Defences in practice}
Twilio's Fraud Guard blocks regions known for pumping~\cite{twilio_fraudguard}; its SMS Pumping Risk Score rates numbers from carrier, traffic and conversion signals and advises friction from 60 and refusal above 90~\cite{twilio_pumping_risk}. Graded destination action is thus established practice, with an unpublished model; no product was benchmarked. CAPTCHA solving is a labour market at about a dollar per thousand~\cite{motoyama2010captcha}, open where machine solvers fail~\cite{dionysiou2020captcha}; client puzzles~\cite{dean2001puzzles} were not evaluated. Residential proxy addresses rotate within minutes and are rarely blacklisted~\cite{mi2019resip}, and carrier-grade NAT makes address reputation on cellular networks ``all but infeasible''~\cite{richter2016cgn}.

\paragraph{Identity and change detection}
One entity can present many identities~\cite{douceur2002sybil}; a signed session gives continuity, not scarcity. Our block tests are Wald's sequential tests~\cite{wald1945sprt} run as Page's cumulative sums~\cite{page1954cusum}. Quickest change detection minimises delay subject to false-alarm constraints~\cite{veeravalli2012qcd}, and a CUSUM's parameters are set from the error rates it must meet~\cite{nist_cusum}; an unconstrained grid is a set of sampled operating points, which is how we report ours. Confidence sequences~\cite{howard2021confseq} and windowed statistics were not evaluated. Step~7 resembles risk-based authentication~\cite{wiefling2019rba}; attacks on the code once sent are a separate problem~\cite{mulliner2013smsotp,siadati2017sms}.

\paragraph{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}
The attacker wants as many messages sent as possible, or sent to numbers that pay it. It buys residential proxies (about 3 USD per gigabyte) and human-like reCAPTCHA tokens (about 0.3 cents), mints fingerprints, generates numbers and knows the thresholds. A pumper also has a colluding operator's ranges; it may enter the codes delivered there, report deliveries as failed, build verified history and buy challenge solutions. It cannot forge an attestation, change which ranges its partner terminates or make a stranger enter a code. Undelivered messages are assumed billed, which favours the attacker.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

No message is paid for before every check passes, and cheap checks run first. Trust comes from evidence the client cannot forge, an attestation, a server-signed session and the fate of earlier codes, though such evidence can be manufactured (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}).

\subsection{The pipeline and the risk score}

A request passes the steps of Table~\ref{tab:steps} until one refuses it. Step~0 takes the platform only from a valid App Attest or Play Integrity result, ignoring the header. Each address limit, per-number slot, budget reservation and per-block counter slot is one atomic Redis operation, released if a later step refuses; the enclosing steps also make separate writes (Section~\ref{sec:method}). Every outcome after Step~0 except the challenge returns one HTTP~200 body padded to 400\,ms.

Step~7 computes
\begin{equation}
s = \sum_i w_i x_i + w_c\,\frac{\max(0,\theta - r_{\min})}{\theta} + w_s\,f + w_m\,i - w_v\,v_n - w_f\,v_f,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where the $x_i$ are request signals (CAPTCHA score, hosting ASN, fingerprint age, session requests, country mismatch, number class), $r_{\min}$ is the lowest conversion ratio over the request's keys (address, subnet, ASN, fingerprint, country, prefix, destination block), $\theta=0.3$, $w_c=25$, $f$ and $i$ flag a flooded key and one whose codes are entered within seconds, and $v_n$, $v_f$ mark a number and fingerprint with verified history; other weights are 5 to 25 points~\cite{otpguard2026}. Tiers start at 20, 40, 60 and 80: \emph{allow}; \emph{delay} (a doubling wait); \emph{challenge} (credits $-20$ when solved); \emph{downgrade} (push or WhatsApp, never SMS); \emph{block}. The conversion penalty lands on keys legitimate users share, so alone it reaches only \emph{delay}; a datacenter address with a fresh fingerprint scores about 47, \emph{challenge}, so datacenter rotation is stopped only while the attacker declines challenges.

\subsection{The feedback loop}
\label{sec:feedback}

A send resolves as \emph{verified} when its code is entered, \emph{failed} after five wrong attempts or two minutes past the delivery receipt, and \emph{undelivered} without a receipt in sixty seconds or with a failure receipt; only the first two feed reputation. A negative receipt marks the send failed and undelivered in one compare-and-set; a later positive receipt reopens it while the code is valid; a late verification reclassifies it, but an issued verdict stands until it expires. Each transition records its effects in the same write and applies them idempotently; a recovery sweep finishes a dead process's work.

\subsection{Destination-block tests}
\label{sec:blocks}

Every key above can be rotated except the destination: a pumper is paid only on its partner's numbers. We key reputation on the 8-digit block (10\,000 numbers) with two log-likelihood-ratio statistics. The conversion statistic assumes users verify with $p_\ell = 0.8$ and a flooder with $p_a = 0.1$: a failure adds $\ln\frac{1-p_a}{1-p_\ell} = 1.50$, a verification $\ln\frac{p_a}{p_\ell} = -2.08$. The speed statistic assumes a machine enters a verified code within five seconds with $q_a = 0.9$ and a person with $q_\ell = 0.2$, since autofill removes the keystroke~\cite{google_smsretriever,wicg_webotp}; it adds $1.50$ or $-2.08$. A verdict is issued, and the statistic reset, when either exceeds $h = \ln 1000 = 6.91$.

The floor sets the detector's memory: $S_t = \max(-ch,\, S_{t-1} + \text{increment})$. Unbounded $c$ is Wald's test and $c = 0$ Page's cumulative sum; with $Y_t = S_t + ch$, any finite $c$ is a zero-floor CUSUM with threshold $(1+c)h$ and head start $ch$, so thresholds and credits form one grid. The default is $c = 1$.

A first verdict makes the block's first-time clients pass a challenge for an hour; a second within the hour moves them off SMS, refusing those without another channel. The stage is decided in the crossing's atomic write. Clients with verified history are exempt; a 24-hour denylist is a comparator. Tests pause on a prefix during a detected carrier outage.

\paragraph{The counter comparator}
The simplest destination policy counts SMS sends per block. Step~5 reads the count and Step~11 reserves a slot atomically only when an SMS is about to go out, so a refused or challenged request and its retry cost nothing. Windows are daily or short and refilling; past the limit the counter refuses, or acts like a verdict (challenge past the limit, non-SMS past twice the limit).

\subsection{Capacity controls}

Step~9 multiplies its caps by 0.25 to 1.5 according to a baseline job comparing each key with its expected rate, learned from the pipeline's hourly counters as a weekly profile or from the closed hours available; with none, the static cap stands. The default job runs every minute. Verified history avoids the adaptive reduction, not the static caps or budget, and the budget bounds SMS count and spend units, not vendor or fallback costs.

\section{An estimate of pumping leakage}
\label{sec:model}

Against the block tests, leakage depends on the blocks a pumper touches. With $B$ blocks reached, rate $\lambda$, $N$ requests and $k = \lceil h/1.50 \rceil = 5$ sends per block before a verdict,
\begin{equation}
L \approx \min\bigl(N,\; kB + \lambda\,\tau\bigr),
\label{eq:leak}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$, the time a send stays unresolved, is about 2.5 minutes if the carrier never verifies and seconds if it verifies at once. It assumes no pre-existing credit on the blocks, no successfully solved challenges, no verified-history exemptions and no outage suspension, and predicts the first verdict, not containment under graded action. Evaluated on each run's observed blocks it checks the per-block mechanism; it is not a forecast, and once $kB > N$ it only says everything leaks.

A carrier verifying a share $\sigma$ of codes with human-like delay gives the conversion statistic an expected increment of $\sigma(-2.08) + (1-\sigma)(1.50)$, zero at $\sigma^{*} = 0.42$. Below it detection is expected, later as $\sigma$ nears $\sigma^*$; above it the drift is away from the threshold, and a verdict in a finite window depends on the floor and on runs of failures.

\section{Implementation and evaluation method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Implementation}
About 3\,700 lines of Python implement the pipeline, feedback loop and adapters (reCAPTCHA, Play Integrity, App Attest with enrolment tested only on synthetic certificate chains, IP intelligence, number lookup, SMS, WhatsApp) on in-memory and Redis backends. Step~11 writes the audit record, counters and OTP entry before calling the sender, so a synchronous callback finds registered state; these are separate writes, and a crash between them leaks reserved slots or resolves the send as undelivered. Of 353 tests, eight run two instances on a real \texttt{redis-server} and check, on that backend only, the hourly ceiling, single claims of a number, callback counting, block-event counting with graded escalation, recovery of a dead process's transition, mixed receipts and compare-and-set. Two interleavings remain open: a correcting receipt landing between the timeout worker's decision and its removal of the timeout leaves the send unresolved, and a late reversal racing a failure can inflate a block's failure count. The single-threaded simulator exercises neither. No live vendor was called.

\paragraph{Simulation}
\label{sec:sim}
The pipeline runs against scripted vendor fakes on a simulated clock. After a ten-minute warm-up (130 for pumping), each run has twenty minutes of attack over legitimate traffic at 20 requests per minute. Simulated users verify 80\% of codes after a lognormal delay (median 22\,s; 2\,s for the 20\% using autofill); 70\% have a WhatsApp-reachable number, registered with the channel selector; 90\% complete a challenge; 20\% come from a population of 5\,000 account holders with a stable fingerprint and a trusted number. Legitimate and attacker requests use separate random streams, so at a seed every scenario, no attack included, offers the same users; tests assert identical trace hashes, immutable cohorts and a full drain. The baseline job is told the legitimate rate except in Section~\ref{sec:rationing}.

Behavioural parameters are assumptions, annotated with any published figure behind them (one provider reports 68\% conversion~\cite{twilio_verify}). The main study uses thirty seeds per attacker and draws pool size, rate and CAPTCHA class per seed; fixing them narrows the farm's interval from [215, 244] to [223, 232] messages, so most spread is attacker variation. Other studies use ten seeds (five for dilution and the 24-hour fallback and design-alternative cells; eight for the outage). Brackets are 95\% percentile-bootstrap intervals, paired per seed for differences. Source caps are lifted or set at three times the legitimate rate.

\paragraph{Predeclared protocol}
A protocol committed before the runs that use it fixes two studies. The \emph{matched comparison} changes only the destination policy on the full pipeline (block conversion still feeds the risk score in every row); within each family and density it selects, on three tuning seeds, the lowest-leakage setting whose completion is within 0.5 points of the default's, and reports it on ten other seeds. The \emph{robustness study} fixes twelve Latin-hypercube points over conversion (0.6--0.9), autofill, human CAPTCHA scores, WhatsApp reachability (0.3--1.0), returning share and legitimate rate; a held-out family of attack rates (60 to 120 per minute), pools (50\,000 to 500\,000 addresses) and heterogeneous legitimate traffic (per-block conversion, poor routes, resends, a burst); and five claims that hold if true in 90\% of cells, three seeds per point. The first run failed claim C1 in 32 of 108 cells, all on its service clause: attack and no-attack runs shared one random stream, so the clause compared different users. We fixed the pairing, left the protocol unchanged and reran every study; the first run is in the artifact. The fix followed a result, and readers should weigh it so.

\paragraph{Designs and metrics}
Feature flags give seven designs: \textbf{v1} (header-trusted platform, static caps), \textbf{v1-corrected} (platform from attestation), \textbf{budget-only} (sessions, static caps, budget), \textbf{block-limit} (sessions, a refusing counter of five sends per block per day), \textbf{conversion-only} and \textbf{speed-only} (v2 minus one block test), and \textbf{v2}. We report leakage; seeds \emph{contained} (leakage at most 5\% of the attack rate from some minute to the window's end, for at least five minutes), a finite-window property; time to containment over contained seeds; completion, challenge and refusal per offered legitimate user; and verdict events inside the window. Requests that met a verdict and never completed are a \emph{descriptive} count; \emph{attributable} loss compares the same requests with the policy enforced and absent, net of the reverse. \subsection{RQ3: where reputation stops separating}
\label{sec:separability}

A farmed-CAPTCHA attacker uses an address and a fingerprint once and shares its country, prefix and residential ASN with every legitimate user, so the penalty its failures earn lands on everyone. The experiments below cover the settings run, not every setting.

\subsection{RQ5: what destination policies cost legitimate users}
\label{sec:fp}

A Monte Carlo of the deployed tests on one block from empty statistics, 20\,000 trials per cell, gives the probability of at least one verdict over 30 legitimate sends: 0.09\% [0.06, 0.14] at 80\% conversion with 20\% autofill, 1.9\% at 65\% conversion and 19.5\% at 50\%; conversion is the sensitive assumption. Over 24 hours of legitimate traffic (ten seeds) the default raises 0.5 verdict events a day on 200 busy blocks at 80\% conversion and 5.8 at 65\%, meeting 27 of about 28\,800 requests; on 1\,000 blocks, 1.6 and 17.9. Claim C4 (at most one verdict per 1\,000 requests at conversion of 0.75 or more) held in 18 of 18 cells, and in 8 of 18 below 0.75, where it was reported, not judged. A thirty-minute outage on one prefix raises 0.6 to 0.8 verdict events, against 1.8 with send-clocked tests.

\subsection{Adaptive attackers, alternatives, economics and overhead}
\label{sec:adaptive}

These are scripted adaptations, not an optimised adversary; their leakages are observations, not worst-case bounds. Pumpers on a known elevated-cost range leak 7 and 20. A datacenter attacker buying every challenge solution leaks 55; low-and-slow attacks leak at their own rate. The trust builder's 500 pairs leak 263 while verifying for ten minutes, then 253 of 289 flood requests. A threshold-aware carrier leaks 560 with no verdict, and a receipt faker 560 (228 with caps on). Receipt-robust tests, counting failed receipts outside an outage, stop the receipt faker at 16 but raise 30 false verdict events a day instead of 1 on 200 blocks with a poor route; a trust budget of eight exempt requests a minute trims three of four trust-builder cells by 23 to 40 messages at no measured cost. Neither touches the threshold-aware carrier. A counter reads no outcomes, but these carriers were not run against it.

In scenario accounting that credits pumpers with a share of the 0.1422 USD retail price and charges an event-level bill (tokens for every session attempt and request, solved challenges, proxy traffic), the attacker breaks even at a share of 0.04 under v1; under v2, at 0.27 for the never-verifying pumper, 0.23 for the challenge buyer and 0.06 for the human-like carrier, while the instant verifier would need 1.39, more than the whole price. Preparation, numbers and carrier contracts are omitted, so these are lower bounds.

On a real Redis (four vCPUs, four workers), a send costs 13.9\,ms at the median and 27.6\,ms at the 99th percentile, with 49 round trips. With the 400\,ms floor and 50\,ms vendor calls, throughput is 68, 113 and 136 requests per second at concurrency 32, 64 and 128, with 0, 7 and 30\% of requests over the floor. Under an adversarial mixture with heavy-tailed vendors and 1\% timeouts, 21 and 63\% exceed the floor at concurrency 32 and 128. With the floor and instantaneous fake vendors, no pair of five outcome classes was distinguished by a Kolmogorov--Smirnov test (smallest $p = 0.32$) and mean differences were under 0.5\,ms: a failure to detect a difference, not proof of none; requests over the floor are distinguishable by construction.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Lessons}
Count on the key the attacker cannot rotate, and do not ask the attacker's carrier whether its traffic is real: outcome-based destination tests were defeated by carriers that verify with human-like delay, fake receipts or know the thresholds, while a short-window send counter held every pumper tested to 6 to 48 messages in twenty minutes at a small attributable cost, given a fallback channel. Reputation separated attackers only on keys they dominated, and rationing, at the tested settings, depended on a fast controller with history.

\paragraph{Limitations}
Everything is simulated and the calibration is assumed; intervals cover seeds, not those choices. The robustness study bounds the claims to its ranges and held-out family, and its pairing repair followed the first run's failure. Some results follow from the generator (premium numbers in a perfect list, a datacenter attacker that declines challenges, a farm with human scores). Matched attributable loss is the false-alarm cost, measured without an attack. Reachability is drawn per request, not per number; returning users have trusted numbers but no fingerprint history. The counter was not tested against spread, long or adaptive pumpers. Two state-machine interleavings remain open, Step~11 is not one transaction, vendor adapters are untested live, and the timing tests exclude heavy tails and 403 responses. Replaying real traffic is the experiment this paper most needs.

\paragraph{Ethics and privacy}
Verdicts challenge people for what strangers sharing their network or block did, and fingerprints are kept for 90 days; deployment under the GDPR or Saudi Arabia's PDPL needs a lawful basis and a route for refused users. No human data was used.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this simulation, under the tested settings, the design contains attacks that carry a signature at little cost to simulated users, and a never-verifying pumper at a cost a closed-form estimate reproduces. It rations, rather than separates, a residential attacker that looks like a person, refusing half of first-time users. Its outcome-based destination tests are defeated by carriers that verify with human-like delay, fake receipts or know the thresholds; a short-window send counter leaked less in total at every density, though more against the instant verifier on busy blocks.
