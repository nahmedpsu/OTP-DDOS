\author[inst1]{Naveed Ahmed\corref{cor1}}

\begin{abstract}
An endpoint that sends a one-time password by SMS to any number it is given will be abused, to waste money (flooding) or to earn part of the termination fee (SMS pumping). Starting from an incident its operator recalls, we evaluate in simulation a twelve-step defence that derives trust from app attestation, signed sessions and verification outcomes, against simpler designs and 25 attacker profiles. Attacks with a signature in the request are contained at little cost to simulated users; a residential attacker with farmed CAPTCHA scores is rationed, not separated, by refusing half of first-time users. On the destination, short-window counters of SMS sends per number block, selected at a benign service target, leaked 6 to 48 messages across four concentrated 20-minute pumping profiles, against 19 to 452 for sequential tests of verification outcomes, at 0.06 to 0.43 points of attack-free completion loss. Under a second protocol, fixed before its runs, the selected counter met its leakage, benign-cost and relative-leakage claims on new seeds and shifted workloads, and failed both claims about service under attack when the pumper shares blocks with real users. It did not stop pumpers that spread over 30 or more blocks, paced themselves to its quota or built trust on random numbers, and it cost real users once their own traffic on a block approached the quota.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Most online services verify a phone number by sending it a code by SMS, from an endpoint reachable before any account exists, at a tenth of a cent to twenty cents a message. A flooder makes the service send to numbers it does not own; a pumper sends to a range whose operator shares the termination fee, turning the service's SMS budget into its income~\cite{mitre_t1496_003}. Twitter's owner said in 2023 that pumping had cost the company about 60 million dollars a year~\cite{commsrisk2023twitter}, and Huh et al.\ found as many attack requests as genuine ones in three months of one large service's logs~\cite{huh2025ait}.

This paper starts from such an incident on a registration flow the author operated, and from the redesign that followed: a twelve-step pipeline with attestation-backed platform exemptions, signed sessions, number checks before a message is paid for, key reputation built from whether codes were entered, a risk score with five responses, and a hard budget. With no production logs, we ask in simulation which attacks it contains against simpler designs, at what cost in completed registrations (RQ1); which layer is responsible (RQ2); when outcome-based reputation separates an attacker from simulated users (RQ3); what rationing costs when it does not (RQ4); and what destination policies cost legitimate users, with and without an attack (RQ5).

The destination became the centre of the study, because a pumper is paid only on its partner's ranges: under a predeclared comparison, a plain counter of sends per number block leaked less than our sequential tests of what happened to the codes, and a second predeclared protocol mapped where that holds and where it fails. We contribute the incident as eight named gaps, the design and an open implementation, measurements of where reputation stops separating and what rationing depends on, a closed-form leakage estimate, both destination-policy comparisons with their failures, and a collateral-damage study.

\section{Background and related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Revenue-share fraud}
In international revenue share fraud, traffic is generated to an advertised number range and the termination fee is split along the route~\cite{sahin2017sok}. Sahin and Francillon's 3.14 million test numbers from twelve providers show the range to be the fraudster's managed asset, about 80\% of ranges being shared across providers~\cite{sahin2021irsf}.

\paragraph{Detecting artificially inflated SMS}
Huh et al.\ trained gradient-boosted trees on 9.4 million SMS authentication logs from nineteen countries, reaching 89.6\% recall at 0.19\% false positives on web traffic~\cite{huh2025ait}. About 73\% of web attacks sent four or fewer requests per identity a day, defeating per-entity limits, and destination signals were the strongest: about 92\% zero conversion for attacks against 21\% for genuine traffic. Their detector learns from labelled traffic and acts per request; our policies are hand-specified per destination block, and theirs was not reproduced.

\paragraph{Defences in practice}
Twilio's Fraud Guard blocks regions known for pumping~\cite{twilio_fraudguard}; its SMS Pumping Risk Score rates numbers from carrier, traffic and conversion signals and advises friction from 60 and refusal above 90~\cite{twilio_pumping_risk}, so graded destination action is established practice; no product was benchmarked. A token bucket separates a sustained rate from a burst allowance; in RFC 2697 an excess bucket fills only when the committed one is full~\cite{rfc2697}. CAPTCHA solving is a labour market at about a dollar per thousand~\cite{motoyama2010captcha}, open where machine solvers fail~\cite{dionysiou2020captcha}; client puzzles~\cite{dean2001puzzles} were not evaluated. Residential proxy addresses rotate within minutes~\cite{mi2019resip}, and carrier-grade NAT makes address reputation on cellular networks ``all but infeasible''~\cite{richter2016cgn}.

\paragraph{Identity and change detection}
One entity can present many identities~\cite{douceur2002sybil}; a signed session gives continuity, not scarcity. Our block tests are Wald's sequential tests~\cite{wald1945sprt} run as Page's cumulative sums~\cite{page1954cusum}. Quickest change detection minimises delay subject to false-alarm constraints~\cite{veeravalli2012qcd}, and a CUSUM's parameters are set from the error rates it must meet~\cite{nist_cusum}; windowed statistics and confidence sequences~\cite{howard2021confseq} were not evaluated. Step~7 resembles risk-based authentication~\cite{wiefling2019rba}; attacks on the code once sent are a separate problem~\cite{mulliner2013smsotp,siadati2017sms}.

\section{The incident and the adapted attacker}

The registration flow sent an SMS to any unregistered number. The first controls, v1, were reCAPTCHA v3 on web requests, one message per number per minute, five requests per address per minute, a country allow-list, a body-length limit, a proxy block and per-platform caps. \paragraph{Threat model}
\label{sec:threat}
The attacker wants as many messages sent as possible, or sent to numbers that pay it. It buys residential proxies (about 3 USD per gigabyte) and human-like reCAPTCHA tokens (about 0.3 cents), mints fingerprints, generates numbers and knows the thresholds. A pumper also has a colluding operator's ranges; it may enter the codes delivered there, report deliveries as failed, build verified history, spread over many blocks and buy challenge solutions. It cannot forge an attestation, use ranges its partner does not terminate, or make a stranger enter a code.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

No message is paid for before every check passes, and cheap checks run first. Trust comes from evidence the client cannot forge, an attestation, a server-signed session and the fate of earlier codes, though such evidence can be manufactured (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}).

\subsection{The pipeline and the risk score}

A request passes the steps of Table~\ref{tab:steps} until one refuses it. Step~0 takes the platform only from a valid App Attest or Play Integrity result, ignoring the header. Each address limit, per-number slot, budget reservation and per-block counter slot is one atomic Redis operation, released if a later step refuses; the enclosing steps also make separate writes (Section~\ref{sec:method}). Every outcome after Step~0 except the challenge returns one HTTP~200 body padded to 400\,ms.

Step~7 computes
\begin{equation}
s = \sum_i w_i x_i + w_c\,\frac{\max(0,\theta - r_{\min})}{\theta} + w_s\,f + w_m\,i - w_v\,v_n - w_f\,v_f,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
where the $x_i$ are request signals (CAPTCHA score, hosting ASN, fingerprint age, session requests, country mismatch, number class), $r_{\min}$ is the lowest conversion ratio over the request's keys (address, subnet, ASN, fingerprint, country, prefix, destination block), $\theta=0.3$, $w_c=25$, $f$ and $i$ flag a flooded key and one whose codes are entered within seconds, and $v_n$, $v_f$ mark a number and fingerprint with verified history; other weights are 5 to 25 points~\cite{otpguard2026}. Tiers start at 20, 40, 60 and 80: \emph{allow}; \emph{delay} (a doubling wait); \emph{challenge} (credits $-20$ when solved); \emph{downgrade} (push or WhatsApp, never SMS); \emph{block}. A datacenter address with a fresh fingerprint and a middling CAPTCHA score lands in \emph{challenge}.

\subsection{The feedback loop}
\label{sec:feedback}

A send resolves as \emph{verified} when its code is entered, \emph{failed} after five wrong attempts or two minutes past the delivery receipt, and \emph{undelivered} without a receipt in sixty seconds or with a failure receipt; only the first two feed reputation. A positive receipt after a negative one reopens the send while its code is valid; a late verification reclassifies it, but an issued verdict stands until it expires. Each transition records its effects in the same compare-and-set and a recovery sweep finishes a dead process's work, so effects apply at most once, and exactly once if the sweep runs within 20 minutes, with one exception (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsection{Destination policies}
\label{sec:blocks}

A pumper is confined to the ranges its partner terminates but can rotate numbers and blocks within them. Destination policies are keyed on the 8-digit block (10\,000 numbers), so their protection weakens as a pumper spreads over more blocks.

\paragraph{Sequential tests}
The conversion statistic assumes users verify with $p_\ell = 0.8$ and a flooder with $p_a = 0.1$: a failure adds $\ln\frac{1-p_a}{1-p_\ell} = 1.50$, a verification $\ln\frac{p_a}{p_\ell} = -2.08$. The speed statistic assumes a machine enters a verified code within five seconds with $q_a = 0.9$ and a person with $q_\ell = 0.2$, since autofill removes the keystroke~\cite{google_smsretriever,wicg_webotp}; it adds $1.50$ or $-2.08$. A verdict is issued, and the statistic reset, when either exceeds $h = \ln 1000 = 6.91$. The floor $S_t = \max(-ch,\, S_{t-1} + \text{increment})$ sets the memory: unbounded $c$ is Wald's test, $c = 0$ Page's cumulative sum, and any finite $c$ a zero-floor CUSUM with threshold $(1+c)h$ and head start $ch$; the default is $c = 1$. A first verdict makes the block's first-time clients pass a challenge for an hour; a second within the hour moves them off SMS, refusing those without another channel. The stage is decided in the crossing's atomic write.

\paragraph{Counters}
A counter limits SMS sends per block in a daily or short refilling window. Step~11 reserves a slot, and decides the stage, in one atomic operation when an SMS is about to go out, so a refused or challenged request costs nothing and concurrent requests cannot skip the challenge. Past the limit a graded counter challenges first-time clients, and past twice the limit moves them off SMS; a refusing counter refuses. Clients with verified history are exempt from both policies, so a code entered on a pumper's number can buy that number an exemption. A token bucket of the same rate (burst 4 or 12, with a challenge-tier bucket that refills independently, unlike RFC~2697's excess bucket) and the counter combined with the sequential tests were run as alternatives.

\subsection{Capacity controls}

Step~9 multiplies its caps by 0.25 to 1.5 according to a baseline job comparing each key with its expected rate, learned from the pipeline's hourly counters as a weekly profile or from the closed hours available; with none, the static cap stands. Verified history avoids the adaptive reduction, not the static caps or budget, and the budget bounds SMS count and spend units, not vendor or fallback costs.

\section{An estimate of pumping leakage}
\label{sec:model}

Against the sequential tests, a pumper reaching $B$ blocks at rate $\lambda$ with $N$ requests leaks about
\begin{equation}
L \approx \min\bigl(N,\; kB + \lambda\,\tau\bigr), \qquad k = \lceil h/1.50 \rceil = 5,
\label{eq:leak}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$, the time a send stays unresolved, is about 2.5 minutes if the carrier never verifies and seconds if it verifies at once. It assumes no pre-existing credit, no solved challenges, no verified-history exemptions and no outage suspension, and predicts the first verdict, not containment under graded action. Evaluated on each run's observed blocks it checks the mechanism; once $kB > N$ it only says everything leaks. A carrier verifying a share $\sigma$ of codes with human-like delay gives the conversion statistic an expected increment of $\sigma(-2.08) + (1-\sigma)(1.50)$, zero at $\sigma^{*} = 0.42$.

\section{Implementation and evaluation method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Implementation}
About 3\,700 lines of Python implement the pipeline, feedback loop and vendor adapters (App Attest enrolment tested only on synthetic certificate chains) on in-memory and Redis backends. Of 393 tests, twelve run two instances on a real \texttt{redis-server}, covering the hourly ceiling, number claims, callback counting, graded escalation, crash recovery, mixed receipts and the counter's boundaries under concurrency; eight concurrent requests at a four-send limit give four SMS and four challenges. These test specific cases, not all interleavings. Known open defects, none exercised by the single-threaded simulator: Step~11's writes after its reservations are separate, so a crash leaks reserved slots or resolves the send as undelivered; a correcting receipt landing between the timeout worker's decision and its removal of the timeout leaves the send unresolved; a late verification recovered more than about thirteen minutes after it arrives can leave the failure it reverses counted; and a request challenged at Step~11 keeps the source-cap slot it used.

\paragraph{Simulation}
\label{sec:sim}
The pipeline runs against scripted vendor fakes on a simulated clock. After a warm-up of ten minutes (30 or 130 for pumping), each run has twenty minutes of attack (60 or 360 where stated) over legitimate traffic at 20 requests per minute. Simulated users verify 80\% of codes after a lognormal delay (median 22\,s; 2\,s for the 20\% using autofill); 70\% of numbers are reachable on WhatsApp, a stable property of the number; 90\% of users complete a challenge; 20\% are returning account holders with a stable fingerprint, a trusted number and verified history. Legitimate and attacker requests use separate random streams, so at a seed every scenario offers the same users, as tests assert.

Behavioural parameters are assumptions, annotated with any published figure behind them, such as one provider's global conversion rate of 68\% or more~\cite{twilio_verify}. The main study uses thirty seeds per attacker (Table~\ref{tab:attackers}) and draws pool size, rate and CAPTCHA class per seed; in a nested design (ten configurations, three seeds each) these draws account for 92\% [80, 98] of the variance of the farm's leakage. Other studies use ten seeds unless stated. Brackets are 95\% percentile-bootstrap intervals, paired per seed for differences; they describe variation under this generator, not uncertainty about real traffic.

\paragraph{Two protocols and their chronology}
The first protocol, committed before its runs, fixed a \emph{matched comparison} and a \emph{robustness study}. The matched comparison changes only the destination policy and, at three calibration settings of legitimate block density, selects on three tuning seeds the setting with the least leakage summed over four concentrated pumpers among those within 0.5 points of the default's completion; the full sequential grid was searched only at the busiest setting. The robustness study fixes twelve Latin-hypercube points over conversion (0.6--0.9), autofill, CAPTCHA scores, WhatsApp reachability (0.3--1.0), returning share and legitimate rate, and a held-out family of attack rates (60 to 120 per minute), pools (50\,000 to 500\,000 addresses) and heterogeneous legitimate traffic, with claims C1--C5 that hold if true in 90\% of cells. Its first run failed C1 in 32 of 108 cells because attack and no-attack runs drew different users; we fixed the pairing, a fix that followed a result, and reran every study.

The second protocol was written after the matched comparison had selected three counters. It froze them and their comparators and tested them on new seeds (300--309), on the held-out family (seeds 6000 and up, six of which had served other robustness points, never selection), on attacked traffic shared with real users, against the adaptive attackers, and across an operating boundary of block load and pumper spread, with claims K1--K5 under the same pass rule; it was committed before its driver and runs. The main evaluation (9\,919 runs) and the counter study (7\,925) are clean invocations whose runs carry the code hash.

\paragraph{Designs and metrics}
Feature flags give seven designs: \textbf{v1} (header-trusted platform, static caps), \textbf{v1-corrected} (platform from attestation), \textbf{budget-only} (sessions, static caps, budget), \textbf{block-limit} (sessions, a refusing counter of five sends per block per day), \textbf{conversion-only} and \textbf{speed-only} (v2 minus one block test), and \textbf{v2}. A seed is \emph{contained} when leakage stays at most 5\% of the attack rate from some minute to the window's end, for at least five minutes. Requests that met a verdict and never completed are a \emph{descriptive} count; \emph{attributable} loss compares the same requests with the policy enforced and absent, net of the reverse, in points of offered users, on attack-free traffic (benign) or on the attacked trace.

\subsection{RQ3: where reputation stops separating}
\label{sec:separability}

A farmed-CAPTCHA attacker uses an address and a fingerprint once and shares its country, prefix and residential ASN with every legitimate user, so the penalty its failures earn lands on everyone.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3_dilution}
\caption{The farm at multiples of the legitimate rate for an hour, caps lifted, five seeds: (a) leaked share per run, intervals narrower than the markers except at 10$\times$; (b) legitimate users challenged and refused.}
\label{fig:dilution}
\end{figure}

Up to twice the legitimate rate, 99 to 100\% of the attack leaks (Figure~\ref{fig:dilution}): a shared key stays above 0.3 conversion until failures outnumber legitimate sends about $(0.8-0.3)/0.3 \approx 1.7$ to one, and the conversion penalty alone reaches only \emph{delay}. At ten times, 70\% leaks while 17\% of legitimate users are challenged and 3.2\% refused. Claim C2 (the farm leaks at least 90\% with caps lifted) held in 34 of 36 cells. Changing the risk score one setting at a time (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}b) separates no better: the conversion weight and window change nothing, and a fresh-fingerprint weight of 40 stops the attacker only by challenging 47\% of legitimate users and refusing 8.5\%, because most sign-ups are new browsers too. In the settings run, reputation separated an attacker only on keys its sends dominated~\cite{douceur2002sybil}.

\subsection{RQ4: what rationing costs, and what it depends on}
\label{sec:rationing}

When separation fails, the cap is the lever (Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff}a): the default (floor 0.25, base three times the legitimate rate) lets 47\% of the farm's requests through and refuses 41\% of all legitimate users; the tightest setting, 20\% and 64\%. \subsection{Adaptive attackers, economics and overhead}
\label{sec:adaptive}

These scripted adaptations are not an optimised adversary; their leakages are not bounds. Against v2, a datacenter attacker buying every challenge solution leaks 55; the trust builder's 500 pairs leak 263 while verifying, then 253 of 289 flood requests; a threshold-aware carrier and a receipt faker leak 570 each with no verdict. Receipt-robust tests stop the receipt faker at 18 but raise 30 false verdict events a day instead of 1 on blocks with a poor route; a trust budget of eight exempt requests a minute trims three of four trust-builder cells by 24 to 44 messages.

If pumpers earn a share of the 0.1422 USD retail price and pay an event-level bill, the attacker breaks even at a share of 0.043 under v1; under v2, at 0.271 (never verifies), 0.235 (buys challenges) and 0.054 (human-like), while the instant verifier would need 1.310; over 360 minutes under the counter the human-like carrier would need 1.048. These rest on assumed prices and omit preparation and contracts; they do not measure criminal profit.

On a real Redis (four vCPUs, four workers), a send costs 13.8\,ms at the median and 21.1\,ms at the 99th percentile. With the 400\,ms floor and 50\,ms vendor calls, throughput is 67, 118 and 133 requests per second at concurrency 32, 64 and 128, with 0.1, 0.7 and 30\% of requests over the floor. With fast vendors no pair of five outcome classes was distinguished (smallest Kolmogorov--Smirnov $p = 0.05$; best single-threshold accuracy 53\%). Under an adversarial mixture with heavy-tailed vendors at concurrency 128, 81\% of sends overran the floor, and an observer seeing only its own response times told a send from a refusal with 88\% balanced accuracy (threshold fitted to 22 refusals).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Lessons}
Outcome-based destination tests were defeated by carriers that verify with human-like delay, fake receipts or know the thresholds. A short-window send counter was not, but it lost to wide, paced and trust-building pumpers and cost users whose own traffic on a block neared its quota or was shared with an attacker. For an operator, the results point to a quota set from each block's own traffic, the counter and tests together, which leaked least, and watching for spread, which neither contains.

\paragraph{Limitations}
Everything is simulated and the calibration is assumed. Both protocols were written by the author for the author's generator, the second knowing which counters had won. Some results follow from the generator (a perfect premium list, a datacenter attacker that declines challenges, a farm with human scores), and selection used an unweighted sum of four scripted pumpers' leakage and a mean service target. The implementation defects of Section~\ref{sec:method} are open, vendor adapters are untested live, and the timing result holds only for the observer model tested. Replaying real traffic is the experiment this paper most needs.

\paragraph{Ethics and privacy}
Destination policies challenge people for what strangers sharing their network or block did, and fingerprints are kept for 90 days; deployment under the GDPR or Saudi Arabia's PDPL needs a lawful basis and a route for refused users. No human data was used.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

In this simulation, at the tested settings, the design contains attacks that carry a signature at little cost to simulated users, and a never-verifying pumper at a cost a closed-form estimate reproduces; it rations, rather than separates, a residential attacker that looks like a person. Counting sends on the destination beat testing outcomes against concentrated pumpers, but a frozen counter failed its service claims when the attacker shares blocks with real users, and loses to wide, paced and trust-building pumpers.
